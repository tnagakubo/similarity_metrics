# FTT Collaborative Group 1994 - Indications for Fibrinolytic Therapy in AMI

## Citation
FTT Collaborative Group (1994) "Indications for fibrinolytic therapy in suspected acute myocardial infarction: collaborative overview of early mortality and major morbidity results from all randomised trials of more than 1000 patients." *Lancet* 343(8893):311-22.
DOI: [10.1016/S0140-6736(94)91161-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(94)91161-4)
PMID: 7905143

## Summary

Meta-analysis of 9 RCTs (N=58,600) of fibrinolytic therapy vs. control in suspected AMI. The primary finding is that fibrinolytic therapy reduces 35-day mortality by 18% (SD 2), corresponding to ~18 deaths avoided per 1000 patients. The main conclusion is that the benefit applies broadly: **"irrespective of age, sex, blood pressure, heart rate, or previous history of myocardial infarction or diabetes."**

The paper identifies an "early hazard" (excess deaths on days 0-1, especially in elderly and late presenters) but this is outweighed by a larger benefit on days 2-35. The authors caution against withholding fibrinolytic therapy on the basis of age, sex, or blood pressure.

## Key Findings by Subgroup

### Age
- **Proportional reduction**: Significant trend toward larger proportional reduction in younger patients (2p=0.01; Figure 1), but effect seen across all age groups.
- **Absolute reduction**: "seem much the same among younger and older patients" (Figure 5, Table 4) — baseline risk is higher in elderly, which partially offsets the smaller proportional benefit.
- **Early hazard**: Excess deaths on days 0-1 increased with age; but so did the benefit during days 2-35.
- **Conclusion**: "The data do not provide evidence for withholding fibrinolytic therapy from patients on the basis of age."
- **NOTE**: No specific per-subgroup mortality reduction numbers (e.g., 1%pt vs 3.4%pt) are given in the text. Subgroup data are presented in Figure 5 (graphical) and Table 4, but specific text-reported numbers are not available for individual age bands.

### Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP)
- **Proportional reduction**: "The proportional reductions in the risk of death did not appear to be much influenced by the SBP or heart rate" (Figure 1).
- **Absolute reduction**: Largest in low-SBP patients: "about 60 lives saved per 1000 patients with SBP under 100 mm Hg" (2p<0.001, Figure 6, Table 4). This is driven by elevated baseline mortality risk in hypotensive patients.
- **Direction of modification**: Low SBP (hypotension) → high absolute benefit; normal-to-high SBP → average-to-lower absolute benefit.
- **Conclusion**: SBP < 100 mmHg should NOT be a contraindication; these patients benefit most.
- **IMPORTANT**: The proportional CATE (treatment effect on relative scale) is NOT strongly modified by SBP. The absolute modification is due to baseline risk differences, not a linear interaction between SBP and drug effect per mmHg.

### Overall mortality reductions by symptom-onset delay
- 0-6 h: ~30 per 1000
- 7-12 h: ~20 per 1000
- 13-18 h: ~10 per 1000 (statistically uncertain)

## Key Implications for nABCD Application Section

### CRITICAL for Age as Effect Modifier
1. FTT does NOT report specific per-age-group mortality numbers (1%pt, 3.4%pt) in the text. These appear to be approximate values from Figure 5 — not directly citable from the text.
2. FTT's main conclusion is "irrespective of age" — the paper does not characterize age as an effect modifier in the traditional sense.
3. A more accurate characterization: "There is a *trend* toward larger proportional benefit in younger patients (FTT, 2p=0.01), but absolute benefits are similar across age groups due to higher baseline risk in elderly patients."
4. To use age as a confirmed effect modifier with estimable L, additional evidence beyond FTT may be needed (e.g., GISSI-1 subgroup, specific interaction analyses).

### CRITICAL for SBP as Effect Modifier
1. FTT explicitly states: "proportional reductions in the risk of death did not appear to be much influenced by the SBP."
2. The large absolute benefit in low-SBP patients reflects baseline risk elevation, not a linear CATE × SBP interaction.
3. Framing SBP as a "candidate effect modifier with weaker evidence" is broadly correct, but the direction of modification in FTT is: **lower SBP → larger benefit** (opposite of what one might assume from "weaker evidence").
4. L* reverse-calculation for SBP is still methodologically valid, but the FTT framing needs careful wording.
