\abstract[Abstract]{%
The ICH E17 guideline describes regional pooling in multi-regional clinical trials (MRCTs) in terms of the similarity of effect modifier distributions but names no measure, and to our knowledge no distance between regional effect modifier distributions has been evaluated for it. We quantify how two regions' distributions of each candidate effect modifier differ by the Wasserstein-1 ($W_1$) distance in original units. If the conditional average treatment effect is common to all regions, depends only on that modifier and is Lipschitz with clinical slope $L_{\text{clinical}}$, regional risk or mean differences differ by at most $\Delta_{\max} = L_{\text{clinical}} W_1$; when $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is unknown, the smallest slope $L^*$ at which the distance could produce a clinical margin is compared with a sponsor's bound. In a selection simulation (matches supplied), the standardized mean difference stayed at chance up to 2,000 patients per region in some configurations; $W_1$ and the Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) statistic reached the target (AUC 0.9) in every configuration, $W_1$ needing fewer patients in \numval{nCellsW1FewerKS} of \numval{nCellsSelection} cells (three by construction), overlapping in one, and more in the location-shift control on contaminated tails. \section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%==============================================================================

Multi-regional clinical trials (MRCTs), conducted across multiple countries or regulatory regions under a single protocol, have become a common design in global pharmaceutical development, and methods for assessing the consistency of treatment effects across their regions have developed with them.\cite{chen2010,quan2010} The International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) E17 guideline, adopted in 2017, established principles for planning and designing MRCTs to evaluate whether treatment effects can be regarded as generalizable across the target population.\cite{iche17}

% Cut 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1 I01, F): CLAIMS C-01, C-18
Regulatory authorities in Japan and China examine the consistency of treatment effects in regional subpopulations,\cite{matsushima2024,song2025} yet individual regional sample sizes in global MRCTs are often insufficient for that purpose. Regional pooling, in which regions with similar patient characteristics are grouped for analysis, is a key strategy for enhancing the assessment of regional consistency, and the ICH E17 guideline describes pooling decisions in terms of the similarity of effect modifier distributions.\cite{iche17}

% Cut 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1 I02, F): CLAIMS C-19
An effect modifier is a baseline patient characteristic, such as age, disease severity or a genetic marker, for which the treatment benefit differs across subgroups. Where it exists, regions with different patient compositions may observe different average treatment effects even if the drug works identically at the individual level: a region with predominantly younger patients would show larger benefits than one with predominantly older patients because the patient mix differs, not the drug. The similarity of the full effect modifier distribution, not only its average level, therefore matters for regional pooling. We focus on continuous effect modifiers such as age and disease severity scores; categorical effect modifiers raise distinct considerations and are outside the present scope.

Despite the regulatory importance of effect modifier distributional similarity, current practice lacks a standardized, clinically calibrated methodology. The ICH E17 guideline provides no specific metric, threshold, or statistical procedure for determining when distributions are ``similar enough.'' Song et al., on the NMPA's implementation of ICH E17, identify the pooling strategy as an area where ambiguity remains and describe, when several effect modifiers are considered, clustering populations on distances defined from the correlation between effect modifiers and endpoints.\cite{song2025} The pooling-strategy chapter of a reference volume on multi-regional trials under ICH E17 introduces, as one of many possible approaches, cluster analysis of regions on region-level summaries of the candidate effect modifiers: each region is given a representative value for each candidate, the values are typically standardized, and distances such as the Euclidean distance are weighted for relevance, for instance through a Lasso regression on the outcome; the chapter notes that the selection of the distance measure is one of the key decisions, and does not evaluate the approach.\cite{komiyama2024} Two gaps remain. First, to our knowledge no distance between regional effect modifier distributions has been proposed and evaluated for this purpose: a single summary per modifier leaves two regions that agree on it but differ in spread, shape, or tail behaviour at distance zero---for a binary characteristic, as in the chapter's illustration, one proportion describes the distribution completely, so the limitation concerns continuous modifiers---and any finite set of summaries requires deciding in advance which distributional features are relevant. Second, the chapter calls for a judgment of whether differences in the impact of effect modifiers across pooled regions are ``clinically meaningful or not'' but supplies no metric to operationalize that judgment.

The ingredients are not new in themselves. The inequality on which the clinical calibration below rests is the Kantorovich--Rubinstein duality, and the same bound on a difference of expectations under a Lipschitz function is used in the optimal-transport analysis of domain adaptation,\cite{shen2018} and a Wasserstein-1 bound of the same family holds under a reproducing-kernel condition;\cite{redko2017} the similarity between a trial sample and a target population has been summarized by propensity-score-based metrics\cite{stuart2011} and by a generalizability index,\cite{tipton2014} within the wider literature on extending trial inferences to a target population;\cite{dahabreh2020} and the similarity of two distributions within a trimmed Mallows-distance neighbourhood has been framed as an equivalence-testing problem.\cite{munk1998} None of these converts the difference between two regions' distributions of an individual effect modifier into a bound on the regional effect difference whose constant is a clinical slope, or evaluates such a distance for regional pooling. The contribution of this paper is the formulation for regional pooling in MRCTs, the reverse calibration $L^*$ when the slope is unknown, and the evaluation of the estimator's identification and estimation properties.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
%==============================================================================

% Cut 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1 M01, F): CLAIMS C-19
Let the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) be denoted $\tau(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]$, where $X$ is the effect modifier. When this function is non-constant, the average treatment effect observed in region $r$ depends on the distribution $F_r$ of the effect modifier in that region:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\tau}_r = \int \tau(x) \, dF_r(x).
\label{eq:regional_ate}
\end{equation}
Assessing pooling suitability therefore requires a measure of distributional difference between regions. Equation~(\ref{eq:regional_ate}), and everything built on it, presupposes an effect scale on which the regional effect is the average of the conditional effects, that is, a collapsible contrast such as a risk difference, a mean difference or a difference in restricted mean survival time; marginal odds and hazard ratios are not averages of their conditional counterparts and fall outside Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity} unless the regional effect is first defined by standardization on a collapsible scale.

\subsection{Existing Approaches and Their Limitations}\label{sec:existing}

The most widely used measure for comparing covariate distributions across groups is the standardized mean difference (SMD), defined as
\begin{equation}
\text{SMD} = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{S_{\text{pooled}}},
\label{eq:smd}
\end{equation}
% Cut 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1 M02, F): CLAIMS C-14 (SMD location-only is kept)
where $\bar{X}_r$ denotes the sample mean in region $r$ and $S_{\text{pooled}}$ is the pooled standard deviation.\cite{austin2011} By construction, SMD captures only differences in location. Two distributions with identical means but markedly different variances---for example, $N(50, 5^2)$ and $N(50, 15^2)$---yield $\text{SMD} = 0$ despite a threefold difference in spread.

Distributional distance measures address this limitation by comparing distributions beyond summary statistics. Empirical distribution function (EDF) tests such as the Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) statistic, $D_{\text{KS}} = \sup_x |F_1(x) - F_2(x)|$, and related measures (Cram\'{e}r--von Mises, Anderson--Darling) quantify differences in the full shape of distributions by comparing CDFs directly. Information-theoretic divergences such as Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence,
\begin{equation}
D_{\text{KL}}(P \| Q) = \int p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \, dx,
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}
take a density-based approach, measuring how one probability distribution differs from another.

Each of these approaches has limitations for comparing effect modifier distributions across regions. SMD captures only location and is blind to variance and shape. EDF-based statistics such as the KS statistic capture full distributional differences but lack a clinically interpretable scale, and the bound on treatment effect heterogeneity they admit is governed by the total variation of the CATE, a constant that is clinically stated only for a monotone CATE (Section~\ref{sec:nabcd_metric}). KL divergence is asymmetric, can diverge to infinity when empirical distributions do not fully overlap, and requires density estimation that is impractical with small regional samples. None of these measures offers a bound on regional treatment effect differences whose constant is a per-unit clinical slope, the kind of quantity that treatment-by-covariate interaction analyses estimate. These gaps motivate a measure that (i) captures distributional features beyond location, (ii) provides a clinically interpretable scale, and (iii) offers such a bound; the Wasserstein-1 distance and its clinical calibration, developed next, satisfy all three.

\subsection{Per-Effect-Modifier Wasserstein-1 Distance}\label{sec:nabcd_metric}

% Paragraph revised 2026-10-11 (D-20261011-11, cold referee M1; Tak's standing instruction of 2026-10-11 23:49, individual veto open until submission): adoption justified by attainment, not by the constant alone
% Paragraph revised 2026-10-12 (D-20261012-2, Louis M3; individual veto open until submission): attainment scoped to the Lipschitz class (the KS bound is attained within its own class)
% Paragraph revised 2026-10-12 (D-20261012-3, REF M1; individual veto open until submission): attainment no longer the reason for adoption (it is Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality restated); three reasons stated
Among distributional distances that could capture the full distributional differences relevant to regional pooling, we adopt the Wasserstein-1 distance ($W_1$) for three reasons: it responds to differences beyond location; the heterogeneity bound it admits (Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity} below) has a per-unit clinical slope $L_{\text{clinical}}$ as its constant; and that bound is valid for every CATE in the class, sharp when the slope is the only restriction placed on the CATE, and tighter, as a function of $W_1$ and the mean difference, when the CATE is also monotone; for any particular CATE it is therefore a conservative upper bound. The $W_1$ distance (sometimes called the Earth Mover's Distance) admits three equivalent representations on the real line, each highlighting a different facet of its interpretation.\cite{villani2009,vallender1974}

Let $F_1$ and $F_2$ denote the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of an effect modifier in two regions. Then
\begin{equation}
W_1(F_1, F_2) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |F_1(x) - F_2(x)| \, dx = \int_0^1 |F_1^{-1}(u) - F_2^{-1}(u)| \, du = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(F_1, F_2)} \int |x - y| \, d\pi(x, y),
\label{eq:wasserstein}
\end{equation}
where $F_r^{-1}$ is the quantile function and $\Pi(F_1, F_2)$ is the set of probability couplings with marginals $F_1$ and $F_2$. The CDF form expresses $W_1$ as the area between the two CDFs (Figure~\ref{fig:nabcd_definition}); the quantile form as the integrated absolute difference of quantile functions; and the Kantorovich form as the minimum expected absolute displacement to transport one distribution onto the other. The three forms are mathematically equivalent.\cite{vallender1974} Unlike the standardized mean difference, $W_1$ responds to differences in variance, skewness, and other distributional features.\cite{panaretos2019}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{fig1_w1_definition.pdf}}
\caption{The Wasserstein-1 distance as the area between cumulative distribution functions, illustrated with a right-skewed Region~1 (gamma distribution) and a symmetric Region~2 (normal distribution). Panel~(A): probability densities. Panel~(B): cumulative distribution functions; the shaded area equals $W_1(F_1, F_2)$.\label{fig:nabcd_definition}}
\end{figure}

$W_1$ carries the units of the underlying effect modifier in each of the three equivalent forms of equation~(\ref{eq:wasserstein}): the CDF form integrates a dimensionless difference of probabilities over $x$, the quantile form differences of quantiles, and the coupling form distances $|x - y|$. $W_1(F_1, F_2)$ is therefore expressed on the original scale of the effect modifier, which supports direct clinical interpretation: a regional $W_1 = 5$~mmHg in systolic blood pressure means that optimally transporting one distribution onto the other moves probability mass an average distance of 5~mmHg. The same logic precludes a meaningful direct comparison of $W_1$ values across effect modifiers measured on different scales, so we restrict attention to per-effect-modifier assessment (Section~\ref{sec:discussion} elaborates on this scope choice).

$W_1$ is a proper metric on the space of probability distributions with finite first moment, satisfying non-negativity, symmetry, identity of indiscernibles, and the triangle inequality.\cite{villani2009} It is also affine-equivariant in the variable units, satisfying $W_1(F_{aX+b}, F_{aY+b}) = |a| \cdot W_1(F_X, F_Y)$ for any $a \neq 0$. Beyond these basic properties, $W_1$ admits a Kantorovich--Rubinstein dual representation\cite{villani2009}
\begin{equation}
W_1(F_1, F_2) = \sup_{\|f\|_{\text{Lip}} \leq 1} \left|\int f \, dF_1 - \int f \, dF_2\right|,
\label{eq:dual}
\end{equation}
which expresses $W_1$ as the largest possible difference in expected values of a 1-Lipschitz function $f$ between the two distributions (the notation $\|f\|_{\text{Lip}} \leq 1$ means $|f(x) - f(y)| \leq |x - y|$ for all $x, y$). If the conditional average treatment effect function $\tau(x)$ is Lipschitz continuous with constant $L_{\text{clinical}}$, so that $|\tau(x) - \tau(y)| \leq L_{\text{clinical}} |x - y|$ for all $x, y$, then $\tau / L_{\text{clinical}}$ is 1-Lipschitz and applying equation~(\ref{eq:dual}) yields the central result of this section:

% Proposition reviewed and approved by Tak 2026-09-26 (Option B of the v6 paragraph review, queue #3): assumption that the CATE is the same in every region and depends on X alone.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:heterogeneity}
\textup{(Connection to heterogeneity).} Suppose the CATE function $\tau$ is the same in every region and depends on the effect modifier $X$ alone. If $\tau$ is Lipschitz continuous with constant $L_{\text{clinical}}$, then for any two regions with effect modifier distributions $F_1$ and $F_2$,
\begin{equation}
|\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2| \leq L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot W_1(F_1, F_2).
\label{eq:heterogeneity_bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply equation~(\ref{eq:dual}) with $f = \tau / L_{\text{clinical}}$, which is 1-Lipschitz by assumption. This yields $\left|\int (\tau / L_{\text{clinical}}) \, d(F_1 - F_2)\right| \leq W_1(F_1, F_2)$. Combining with equation~(\ref{eq:regional_ate}) for $\bar{\tau}_r$ and rearranging yields equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}).
\end{proof}

\noindent Equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) is not the only bound of its form. Integration by parts gives $\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2 = -\int (F_1(x) - F_2(x)) \, d\tau(x)$, so for a CATE of bounded variation on a bounded support, $|\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2| \leq \mathrm{TV}(\tau) \cdot D_{\text{KS}}$ as well, and $W_1 \leq W_2$ makes $L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot W_2$ a valid but looser bound.\cite{villani2009} Equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) is sharp when the Lipschitz constant is the only restriction placed on the CATE: by the Kantorovich--Rubinstein duality of equation~(\ref{eq:dual}), $L_{\text{clinical}} W_1$ is the supremum of $|\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2|$ over all common $L_{\text{clinical}}$-Lipschitz CATEs, attained by $\tau^*(x) = L_{\text{clinical}} \int_{x_0}^{x} \operatorname{sgn}(F_2(t) - F_1(t))\,dt$, whose slope changes sign at every crossing of the two CDFs; the Kolmogorov--Smirnov bound is likewise sharp within its own class (Appendix~\ref{app:ks_bound}). Sharpness over a class is a property of the class, and shape information available to the sponsor tightens the bound. If $\tau$ is also monotone, with $0 \leq \tau' \leq L_{\text{clinical}}$, then $\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2 = \int (F_2 - F_1)\,\tau'\,dx$ lies between $-L_{\text{clinical}} A_-$ and $L_{\text{clinical}} A_+$, where $A_\pm = \int (F_2 - F_1)_\pm\,dx$ satisfy $A_+ + A_- = W_1$ and $A_+ - A_- = \mu_1 - \mu_2$, so that
\begin{equation}
\sup_{\tau\ \text{monotone},\ L_{\text{clinical}}\text{-Lipschitz}} |\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2| = L_{\text{clinical}} \max(A_+, A_-) = \tfrac{1}{2} L_{\text{clinical}} \left( W_1 + |\mu_1 - \mu_2| \right),
\label{eq:mono_bound}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Estimation}\label{sec:estimation}

% Citation-only correction 2026-09-27 (Louis, v6 review of queue #48-49): the real-line bootstrap statements are sourced to Fang and Santos 2019 and Dumbgen 1993; Sommerfeld and Munk 2018 covers finite supports only and is kept for that case.

Given samples $\{X_{1,i}\}_{i=1}^{n_1}$ from region 1 and $\{X_{2,j}\}_{j=1}^{n_2}$ from region 2, the Wasserstein-1 distance is estimated using empirical distribution functions. Since $\hat{F}_1$ and $\hat{F}_2$ are piecewise constant (right-continuous step functions), the integral in equation~(\ref{eq:wasserstein}) reduces to a finite sum over the intervals between consecutive distinct values of the pooled sample:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{W}_1 = \sum_{k=1}^{m-1} |\hat{F}_1(x_{(k)}) - \hat{F}_2(x_{(k)})| \cdot (x_{(k+1)} - x_{(k)}),
\label{eq:estimator}
\end{equation}

% Cut 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1 M07, F): CLAIMS C-04
% Cut 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1 M08, F): CLAIMS C-22, C-23
% Cut 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1 M15, F): CLAIMS C-22
% Cut 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1 M16, F): CLAIMS C-22
where $\hat{F}_r$ denotes the empirical CDF (right-continuous) of region $r$ and $x_{(1)} < \cdots < x_{(m)}$ are the $m$ distinct values of the pooled sample (ties are pervasive for coarsely recorded modifiers, such as systolic blood pressure in whole mmHg in Section~\ref{sec:application}). The asymptotic distribution of $W_1$ is non-standard: del Barrio et al.\cite{delbarrio1999} showed that, when $\int \sqrt{F(1-F)}\,dx < \infty$, $\sqrt{n}\,W_1(\hat{F}_n, F) \xrightarrow{d} \int |B(F(x))|\,dx$, where $B$ is a standard Brownian bridge. Its quantiles depend on the unknown $F$, so no universal critical values are available. In the two-sample case a Gaussian limit, and hence a Wald interval, exists under the measure-zero condition stated below; the same condition governs the consistency of the bootstrap, so the choice between them rests on finite-sample behaviour: the Wald interval requires an estimate of the limit's variance, which depends on $\operatorname{sgn}(F_1 - F_2)$ and hence on where the CDFs cross, whereas the percentile bootstrap requires no variance estimate. We therefore employ the nonparametric percentile bootstrap with $B = 2{,}000$ replicates.\cite{efron1993} Each bootstrap replicate draws $n_1$ observations with replacement from region 1 and, independently, $n_2$ from region 2, and recomputes $\widehat{W}_1$ by equation~(\ref{eq:estimator}); the 95\% interval is formed by the 2.5th and 97.5th sample percentiles of the $B$ replicates (R's default quantile definition, type 7). Under the conditions of Appendix~\ref{app:asymptotic}---integrable $\sqrt{F_r(1 - F_r)}$, a sample ratio $n_1/(n_1 + n_2)$ away from 0 and 1, and $\{x : F_1(x) = F_2(x) \in (0,1)\}$ of Lebesgue measure zero, as when the two CDFs cross only at isolated points---the Hadamard derivative of the $L_1$ functional at $F_1 - F_2$ is linear and the ordinary nonparametric bootstrap is consistent by the functional delta method;\cite{fang2019} where the CDFs coincide on an interval, including $F_1 = F_2$, the derivative is non-linear and the naive bootstrap is not consistent,\cite{dumbgen1993,fang2019,sommerfeld2018} and convergence occurs at rate $\sqrt{n_1 n_2/(n_1+n_2)}$. The estimator~(\ref{eq:estimator}) is computed in $O((n_1 + n_2) \log(n_1 + n_2))$ time, dominated by sorting the combined order statistics.

Because $W_1$ is a distance, $\widehat{W}_1$ is non-negative by construction and is typically positive even when the two regions are draws from the same distribution. More precisely, it is biased upward at every sample size, $E\,\widehat{W}_1 \geq W_1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:proof1}), and the percentile interval does not correct this bias, so near the boundary the whole interval is displaced upward (Section~\ref{sec:sim_results}). For the patients actually enrolled, on the other hand, the bound holds exactly with the empirical distributions: the difference between the two enrolled samples' average treatment effects is at most $L_{\text{clinical}}\,W_1(\hat{F}_1, \hat{F}_2)$, whatever the sampling error of $\widehat{W}_1$ as an estimate of $W_1$. We refer to the sampling distribution of $\widehat{W}_1$ under $F_1 = F_2$ as the null floor. It is obtained by drawing two independent resamples, of the two regions' actual sizes, from one region's empirical distribution---the anchor's in Section~\ref{sec:app_operability}---and recomputing $\widehat{W}_1$; because it depends on both sample sizes and on the source distribution, it is computed per region pair. An observed $\widehat{W}_1$ at or below its 95th percentile, the reference point used in Section~\ref{sec:app_operability}, is one the estimator would produce with appreciable probability from identical distributions, so it is not evidence of a difference; how large the difference could be is read from the upper confidence limit of $\widehat{W}_1$, equivalently of $\Delta_{\max}$. Declaring a distance unresolved in this way is a one-sided test of $F_1 = F_2$ at level 0.05; it is used only to mark which estimates are read at their upper limit, and the comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:application} are not adjusted for multiplicity, since no inference about pooling is drawn from the number of unresolved distances.

\subsection{Interpretation and Clinical Calibration}\label{sec:inference}

We refer to the Lipschitz constant of the CATE function $\tau(x)$ as the clinical slope $L_{\text{clinical}}$. By definition, $L_{\text{clinical}}$ quantifies the maximum change in the treatment effect per unit change in the effect modifier, expressed on the outcome scale of the trial---for example, absolute 30-day mortality risk per year of age, or per mmHg of systolic blood pressure. Throughout this paper $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is quoted on the proportion scale of the outcome, so that $L_{\text{clinical}} = 1 \times 10^{-2}$ per year denotes one percentage point of absolute 30-day mortality per year of age; clinical margins $\Delta_{\text{clin}}$ stated in percentage points are divided by 100 before entering any calculation. When prior individual patient data (IPD) are available, meta-regression on treatment-by-covariate interaction estimates the average interaction slope.\cite{riley2010,fisher2017} An average slope is, in absolute value, a lower bound on the Lipschitz constant rather than the constant itself: for non-linear $\tau$, the local slope can exceed the fitted one. An $L_{\text{clinical}}$ specified from such an estimate should therefore carry a sponsor-specified multiplicative safety factor, in the spirit of margin discounting in non-inferiority design, so that the premise of Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity} remains defensible. More commonly at the MRCT planning stage, published subgroup reports document the direction of interaction rather than a per-unit slope, and a precise numerical $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is unavailable. We address both situations below.

From Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity}, the maximum potential difference in regional average treatment effects attributable to effect modifier distributional differences is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\max} = L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot W_1(F_1, F_2),
\label{eq:delta_max}
\end{equation}
expressed in the units of the trial outcome scale. The formula is invariant to within-effect-modifier scale normalization of $W_1$: any normalized form $W_1 / X$ multiplied by the same scale $X$ cancels in equation~(\ref{eq:delta_max}), so adopting $W_1$ in its original units yields the same clinical-scale calibration as any rescaled version. When $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is independently known, equation~(\ref{eq:delta_max}) provides a direct clinical-scale translation of the observed distributional difference. When $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is unknown, the relation can be inverted to ask which $L_{\text{clinical}}$ would be required for a given $W_1$ to produce a clinically meaningful $\Delta_{\max}$.

% Paragraph reviewed 2026-09-27 (Option C, Harvey's recommendation under Tak's standing instruction; v6 paragraph review, queue #8): L* interval defined here once; "full range of evidence states" narrowed to the two evidence states.
The estimator $\widehat{W}_1$ has a percentile bootstrap CI (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}), and inferential uncertainty propagates linearly to $\Delta_{\max}$ when $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is treated as fixed: $[\Delta_{\max,L}, \Delta_{\max,U}] = L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot [W_{1,L}, W_{1,U}]$. When $L_{\text{clinical}}$ itself is uncertain or unavailable, we solve equation~(\ref{eq:delta_max}) for $L_{\text{clinical}}$ at a pre-specified clinical margin $\Delta_{\text{clin}}$ (the minimal clinically important treatment effect difference, the non-inferiority margin, or the targeted overall effect), yielding the required clinical slope
\begin{equation}
L^* = \frac{\Delta_{\text{clin}}}{W_1(F_1, F_2)}.
\label{eq:lstar}
\end{equation}
% Cut 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1 M10, F): CLAIMS C-06
$L^*$ is the smallest clinical slope under which the observed distributional difference could produce a clinically meaningful regional treatment effect difference, within the class of CATEs restricted only by their slope. Because $L^*$ is a decreasing function of $W_1$, the bootstrap CI of $\widehat{W}_1$ gives the percentile interval $[\Delta_{\text{clin}}/W_{1,U},\, \Delta_{\text{clin}}/W_{1,L}]$ for $L^*$. If a plausible upper bound on $L_{\text{clinical}}$ from prior evidence (denoted $L_{\text{UB}}$) lies below $L^*$, the distributional difference cannot produce such a difference, provided $L_{\text{clinical}} \leq L_{\text{UB}}$; reading $L_{\text{UB}}$ against the lower limit $\Delta_{\text{clin}}/W_{1,U}$ rather than the point value carries the estimation uncertainty of $\widehat{W}_1$ into this comparison.

The pooling assessment proceeds by computing per-effect-modifier $W_1$ with bootstrap CIs across candidate partner regions, ordering the candidates by the point estimate $\widehat{W}_1$ within each effect modifier, reading each distance against its null floor and its upper confidence limit (Section~\ref{sec:estimation})---distances unresolved against the null floor are not ordered among themselves---and deriving $\Delta_{\max}$ when $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is known or $L^*$ at a pre-specified $\Delta_{\text{clin}}$ when $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is unknown. Study 1 follows the ADEMP structure (aims, data-generating mechanisms, estimands, methods, performance measures) for simulation studies.\cite{morris2019} Four worlds are simulated, each built from a single distributional family and each containing regions constructed either from the anchor's own population (true matches) or from a discordant population. Set 1 (Gaussian) places discordance in location and scale. Set 2 (log-normal) places it in location and in dispersion combined with shape. Set 3 (mixture) is the adversarial world for summary-based measures: every region, matched or discordant, is constructed with mean 50 and SD 10, so all discordance lies in distributional shape. Set 4 (extremes) places a 5\% subgroup in each tail, 40 units from the centre in the anchor; discordant regions displace both subgroups (to 70 or 100 units), raise their prevalence to 12\% (mean fixed), or displace only the upper subgroup to 100 units; two control regions instead shift their whole population by 2 and 3 units. In the three Set 4 cells in which a subgroup is displaced (symmetric and asymmetric severity) or its prevalence raised, the two full-distribution measures respond differently by construction: a subgroup of prevalence $\varepsilon$ displaced by $d$ changes the KS statistic by at most about $\varepsilon$ whatever $d$ is, while it changes $W_1$ by $\varepsilon d$, and mass added 40 units from the centre moves $W_1$ by that distance per unit of added mass, so $W_1$'s sample-size advantage in those cells follows from the mechanism, and the bulk-shift control, a location shift of the same population, is the cell that removes it. Ground truth is the construction label---which regions were built from the anchor's population---so the target is defined without reference to any of the compared measures and the comparison is not circular.

The measures are scored against the construction label on a selection task. An anchor and nine candidate regions are drawn; each measure ranks the candidates by their distance to the anchor and retains the $k$ closest, where $k$ is the number of true matches. Giving every measure the same $k$ removes threshold choice from the comparison, though supplying $k$ is itself an oracle that the application does not use (Section~\ref{sec:application}). Performance is summarized by the AUC for discriminating true matches from discordant candidates (chance = 0.5) and by the probability that the $k$ retained regions include at least one discordant region. The task ran 10{,}000 replications per cell at the base grid ($n = 25$--100 per region) and 3{,}000 at the extended grid ($n = 150$--2{,}000). The extended grid, the accuracy target and the definitions of the two behaviours below were fixed after the base-grid results had been seen, and the Monte Carlo intervals of Table~\ref{tab:study2_required_n_mc} were added later; the reported target is the middle of the three examined.

The calibration rests on the Lipschitz premise of Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity}, and its limitations are those of the premise. First, the constant is a clinical judgment, informed but not determined by prior evidence (Section~\ref{sec:inference}), so conclusions drawn from $\Delta_{\max}$, or from the comparison of $L^*$ with $L_{\text{UB}}$, are conditional on it; the same holds for an $L_{\text{clinical}}$ taken from an estimated average interaction slope, a lower bound on the Lipschitz constant, for which the safety factor of Section~\ref{sec:inference} is a judgment the framework does not supply. Second, for a CATE that changes steeply over a narrow range of the effect modifier---a threshold or step function---the Lipschitz constant is large and equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) is correspondingly loose. This is by design: the bound is a worst case over the CATEs compatible with the stated $L_{\text{clinical}}$, and a loose bound overstates rather than understates the potential difference; the Kolmogorov--Smirnov alternative for that case is stated in Section~\ref{sec:nabcd_metric}. The requirement is also local: since $\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2 = -\int (F_1 - F_2)\,d\tau$, the bound holds with $L_{\text{clinical}}$ replaced by the Lipschitz constant of $\tau$ on $\{x : F_1(x) \neq F_2(x)\}$, so saturation of the CATE outside that range does not loosen it, although for distributions with full support, such as normal distributions, that range is the whole line. Finally, the bound is per effect modifier: for a CATE additive across modifiers, $\tau(x) = \sum_k \tau_k(x_k)$, the per-modifier bounds combine as $|\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2| \leq \sum_k L_k W_1^{(k)}$ and each per-modifier $\Delta_{\max}$ is the contribution attributable to that modifier, whereas under interaction between modifiers they do not, because marginal distances do not control the joint distance. The joint analogue is immediate: if the CATE is Lipschitz with respect to the weighted metric $d(x, y) = \sum_k L_k |x_k - y_k|$, then $|\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2| \leq W_d(P_1, P_2)$, the Wasserstein distance between the joint distributions under cost $d$, with or without interaction, and since every coupling of the joint distributions couples each pair of marginals, $W_d \geq \sum_k L_k W_1^{(k)}$: the sum of the per-modifier bounds is a lower bound on the joint one, so that under interaction the per-modifier assessment can understate the heterogeneity. Estimating $W_d$ is a multivariate problem outside the scope of this paper.

\bmsection{Proofs and Mathematical Details\label{app:proofs}}

\bmsubsection{Non-negativity and Identity of Indiscernibles\label{app:proof1}}

Non-negativity is immediate: $W_1(F_1, F_2) = \int |F_1(x) - F_2(x)| \, dx \geq 0$, since the integrand is non-negative.

For the identity of indiscernibles, we show both directions. If $F_1 = F_2$, then $|F_1(x) - F_2(x)| = 0$ for all $x$, so $W_1(F_1, F_2) = 0$. Conversely, suppose $W_1(F_1, F_2) = \int |F_1(x) - F_2(x)| \, dx = 0$. Because the integrand is non-negative, it vanishes for Lebesgue-almost every $x$, so $F_1 = F_2$ almost everywhere. Right-continuity then upgrades this to equality everywhere: a null set contains no interval, so for any $x$ and every $\delta > 0$ the interval $(x, x + \delta)$ contains points at which $F_1 = F_2$; choosing such $x_j \downarrow x$ and letting $j \to \infty$ gives $F_1(x) = F_2(x)$. Hence $F_1 = F_2$.

% Moved from Section 2.3 on 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1, needs Tak's veto), verbatim: the pointwise-Jensen derivation of the upward bias of $\widehat{W}_1$.
Upward bias of $\widehat{W}_1$. Since $E\,\hat{F}_r(x) = F_r(x)$, Jensen's inequality gives $E\,|\hat{F}_1(x) - \hat{F}_2(x)| \geq |F_1(x) - F_2(x)|$ at every $x$, and integrating (the integrand is non-negative, so no further condition is needed) $E\,\widehat{W}_1 \geq W_1$ for all $n_1, n_2$.

\bmsubsection{Asymptotic Properties\label{app:asymptotic}}

Under standard regularity conditions, the empirical Wasserstein-1 distance $\widehat{W}_1 = W_1(\hat{F}_1, \hat{F}_2)$ is a consistent estimator of $W_1(F_1, F_2)$.\cite{delbarrio1999}

In one dimension, the empirical $W_1$ distance converges at rate $O(n^{-1/2})$ if and only if $\int \sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))}\,dx < \infty$.\cite{delbarrio1999} For two samples with $n_1/(n_1+n_2) \to \lambda \in (0,1)$, when $\int \sqrt{F_r(1-F_r)}\,dx < \infty$ ($r = 1, 2$) and $\{x : F_1(x) = F_2(x) \in (0,1)\}$ has Lebesgue measure zero, the directional derivative of $W_1$ at $(F_1, F_2)$ is linear, which is the condition under which the bootstrap statement below holds;\cite{fang2019} this condition excludes $F_1 = F_2$. At the boundary $F_1 = F_2$ the derivative is non-linear and the limit is not Gaussian; separately, a percentile interval cannot contain zero, which is the degenerate coverage observed under the null (S1; Section~\ref{sec:sim_results}). For an effect modifier recorded on a grid, the CDFs are step functions and the condition fails whenever they agree at any grid point inside the support; the finite-space results of Sommerfeld and Munk then apply.\cite{sommerfeld2018} Their limit, a maximum over the dual solutions, reduces to a Gaussian when the dual solution is unique, which fails if the two population CDFs coincide on some interval between consecutive grid values. Because the condition concerns the population CDFs, it cannot be checked from the samples; for a distance unresolved against its null floor, such as R2 on systolic blood pressure in Section~\ref{sec:application}, $F_1 = F_2$ is not excluded and the interval is read at its upper limit only (Section~\ref{sec:app_operability}).

\bmsubsection{Comparison with the Kolmogorov--Smirnov Bound; Attainment on the Outcome Scale\label{app:ks_bound}}

% Moved from Section 2.2 on 2026-10-12 (D-20261012-3, EXT Minor 5): the technical comparison of the two bounds.
Where both constants are available, neither bound dominates in general. On a support $[a, b]$, $W_1 \leq (b - a)\,D_{\text{KS}}$ and $\mathrm{TV}(\tau) \leq L_{\text{clinical}}\,(b - a)$, and $L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot W_1 \leq \mathrm{TV}(\tau) \cdot D_{\text{KS}}$ holds exactly when $W_1 / D_{\text{KS}} \leq \mathrm{TV}(\tau) / L_{\text{clinical}}$. For a CATE whose slope has constant magnitude $L_{\text{clinical}}$, such as a linear CATE, $\mathrm{TV}(\tau) = L_{\text{clinical}}(b - a)$ and equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) is never the looser of the two; the Kolmogorov--Smirnov bound gains as the CATE's slope falls below its maximum over much of the support, most sharply when the CATE changes steeply over a narrow range---the threshold case in which equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) is itself conservative (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Every valid bound with the constant $L_{\text{clinical}}$, including $L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot W_2$ and, on $[a, b]$, $L_{\text{clinical}} (b - a) D_{\text{KS}}$, is at least $L_{\text{clinical}} W_1$, and the $L^*$ of Section~\ref{sec:inference} is the smallest slope at which a CATE restricted only by its slope produces the margin.

% Moved from Section 2.2 on 2026-10-14 (D-20261014-1, needs Tak's veto), verbatim: the sharpness of the Kolmogorov--Smirnov bound within its own class, and the bounded-support / unbounded-support caveat with the Lee et al. comparison.
The Kolmogorov--Smirnov bound is likewise sharp within its own class, by a step CATE of height $\mathrm{TV}(\tau)$ at the point where $|F_1 - F_2|$ is largest. A bound on the magnitude of $\tau$ itself tightens equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) further: on a support $[a, b]$ the attaining $\tau^*$ ranges over up to $L_{\text{clinical}} (b - a)$, which on the risk-difference scale is \numval{lipRangeAge} for age and \numval{lipRangeSbp} for systolic blood pressure over the recorded supports of Section~\ref{sec:application} (supports that include the records flagged there), exceeding the highest 30-day mortality Lee et al.\ report for any age group (20.5\% above 75 years\cite{lee1995}), so that the attaining CATE would require effects far beyond the baseline risks; for distributions with unbounded support no CATE bounded on the outcome scale attains the bound at all. The 30-day mortality that Lee et al.\ report for GUSTO-I is 1.1\% below 45 years and 20.5\% above 75 years,\cite{lee1995} a rise of 19.4 percentage points over at least three decades and hence an average prognostic age gradient of at most about $6.5 \times 10^{-3}$ per year (Section~\ref{sec:inference}).

\bmsection{R Code for $W_1$ Computation\label{app:rcode}}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={R function for computing the Wasserstein-1 distance with bootstrap confidence intervals.},label=lst:nabcd,basicstyle=\fontsize{8}{10}\selectfont\ttfamily]
compute_W1 <- function(x1, x2) {
  pooled <- c(x1, x2)
  all_vals <- sort(unique(pooled))
  left <- head(all_vals, -1)
  F1 <- ecdf(x1); F2 <- ecdf(x2)
  w1 <- sum(diff(all_vals) * abs(F1(left) - F2(left)))
  w1
}

W1_bootstrap <- function(x1, x2,
    B = 2000, conf = 0.95) {
  obs <- compute_W1(x1, x2)
  boot_vals <- replicate(B, {
    b1 <- sample(x1, replace = TRUE)
    b2 <- sample(x2, replace = TRUE)
    compute_W1(b1, b2)
  })
  alpha <- 1 - conf
  ci <- quantile(boot_vals,
    c(alpha/2, 1 - alpha/2), na.rm = TRUE)
  list(estimate = obs,
       ci_lower = ci[1], ci_upper = ci[2])
}
\end{lstlisting}
