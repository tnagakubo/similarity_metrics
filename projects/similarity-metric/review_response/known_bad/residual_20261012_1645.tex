\section{Fixture}
Among distributional distances that could capture the full distributional differences relevant to regional pooling, we adopt the Wasserstein-1 distance ($W_1$) because its heterogeneity bound (Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity} below) is attained within the class defined by the per-unit clinical slope $L_{\text{clinical}}$: among the bounds with that constant, it is the smallest valid one.

In the bulk-shift control the KS statistic reaches the target first (333 versus 377), whereas in the uniform-shift cells of Sets 1 and 2 $W_1$ does (33 versus 39; 129 versus 157): the control removes $W_1$'s Set 4 advantage rather than reversing the order in general, and we report the loss. At the AUC 0.9 target the 95\% intervals place $W_1$ below the KS statistic in 11 cells. The reason to use $W_1$ is therefore not that it always gives a different answer, but that, like the KS statistic, it recovers in every world, while reaching the selection target with fewer patients than the KS statistic in 11 of 13 cells at AUC 0.9, three of them by construction.

The undercoverage for S2 at small sample sizes (0.703 at $n=50$) and S5 (0.862 at $n=50$) reflects boundary proximity effects that diminish with increasing $n$. A measure is recovering when its AUC rises monotonically through the target. In the two-sample case a Gaussian limit, and hence a Wald interval, exists under the measure-zero condition stated below, but the condition cannot be verified from data and the limit's variance involves $\operatorname{sgn}(F_1 - F_2)$.

The orderings by age and by systolic blood pressure distance do not agree (Spearman correlation $-0.30$). At $n = 100$ in Set 4 (all contrast types pooled), the probability that the three retained regions include at least one discordant region is 0.718 for $W_1$ and 0.971 for the KS statistic.
