# Referee report — Quantifying Effect Modifier Similarity for Regional Pooling in Multi-Regional Clinical Trials
Target: Statistics in Medicine. Reviewed against `paper/per_em_W1_wiley.tex` (708 lines, commit fad1010 plus uncommitted working-tree changes, md5 4e131b4a3a76075ad7f89b7963ce5ca7) and the recorded outputs in `results/` and `data/`. Line numbers below are source lines of that file.

## Overall assessment

The paper proposes the per-effect-modifier Wasserstein-1 distance, with percentile-bootstrap intervals, to quantify how regional distributions of a continuous effect modifier differ in an MRCT. It calibrates the distance clinically through the Kantorovich–Rubinstein bound $|\bar\tau_1-\bar\tau_2|\le L_{\text{clinical}}W_1$, and through its inverse $L^*=\Delta_{\text{clin}}/W_1$ when the slope is unknown. Two simulation studies back it up: Study 1 checks whether each measure can tell regions with the anchor's distribution from regions with a different one; Study 2 checks bias and coverage. A GUSTO-I worked example illustrates the method. The scope is stated carefully. The paper quantifies differences and does not prescribe pooling. It is candid about the null boundary (S1), the null floor, and the post hoc parts of Study 1 (extended grid, target, the Monte Carlo intervals added later). The arithmetic is sound. I recomputed every application number from `data/gusto.csv`: W1 point estimates match to 1e-15, CIs and rank intervals match within bootstrap error, the null-floor classification gives the same six age partners and R2 on SBP, the L* identities hold, and the Spearman values match. Every cell of Tables 2–5 also matches the summary CSVs. Proposition 1, the integration-by-parts identity, the TV·KS bound, the additive-CATE extension and the identity-of-indiscernibles proof are all correct.

I would reject in the current form for one reason, and it sits at the centre of the motivation. The manuscript chooses $W_1$ "because its heterogeneity bound … is attained within the class … it is the smallest valid one" (l.136), and repeats this as the principal contribution (l.570, l.589, l.623). That property is a restatement of Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality for the unconstrained Lipschitz class, and it is attained only by a zig-zag CATE. In the evidence state the paper itself calls typical (subgroup reports give the direction of interaction, l.211), the sharp bound over monotone $L$-Lipschitz CATEs is $L(W_1+|\mu_1-\mu_2|)/2$, not $LW_1$. That is half of $LW_1$ for every mean-matched contrast, including S5 and S6 and the mixtures of Set 3. Under the bounded-effect constraint of the paper's own risk-difference scale, the attaining CATE swings by 91 percentage points over the GUSTO-I age range. So "attained / smallest valid" is true only as stated for the unrestricted class. As a reason to prefer $W_1$ it is circular: the Lipschitz class is chosen, and then $W_1$ is the bound for that class by definition. The defensible version is easy to write (M1). Separately, the "11 of 13 cells" efficiency tally in the abstract counts cells of a designed grid. The one cell that reverses the order does so for a general reason (tail weight inflates $\widehat W_1$'s sampling variance but not the KS statistic's), and the manuscript does not state it (M2).

## Major

### M1. "Attained / smallest valid bound" is true only for the unrestricted Lipschitz class. Under monotonicity, the paper's own typical evidence state, the sharp bound is $L(W_1+|\Delta\mu|)/2$
§2.2, l.136 ("we adopt … $W_1$ because its heterogeneity bound … is attained within the class … it is the smallest valid one"); l.130; l.181 (attainment argument); l.570, l.589, l.623 (principal contribution).

(a) *What the attainment statement is.* $\sup\{|\int\tau\,d(F_1-F_2)|:\ \|\tau\|_{\rm Lip}\le L\}=LW_1$ is the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality (eq. 6). Fix the function class and the integral probability metric that is sharp for it is fixed too. Calling this an advantage of $W_1$ over the KS bound is circular. The manuscript concedes at l.181 that the KS bound "is likewise attained within its own class". The substantive choice is the class, and the paper does not argue for the class against its clinically motivated subclasses.

(b) *Monotone CATEs: derivation.* Let $\tau$ be non-decreasing with $0\le\tau'\le L$. Then $\bar\tau_1-\bar\tau_2=\int(F_2-F_1)\tau'\,dx\in[-L A_-,\,L A_+]$ with $A_+=\int(F_2-F_1)_+$ and $A_-=\int(F_1-F_2)_+$. Since $A_++A_-=W_1$ and $A_+-A_-=\mu_1-\mu_2$,
$$\sup_{\tau\ \text{monotone},\ L\text{-Lip}}|\bar\tau_1-\bar\tau_2| = L\,\max(A_+,A_-) = \tfrac{L}{2}\big(W_1+|\mu_1-\mu_2|\big),$$
attained by the monotone CATE with slope $L$ on $\{F_2>F_1\}$ (or on $\{F_1>F_2\}$) and zero elsewhere. If the direction is known, the bound is the one-sided $LA_+$ or $LA_-$. Consequences:
- For S5 and S6 (mean-matched) and for every mean-matched cell of Studies 1–2, the sharp monotone bound is exactly $LW_1/2$. For S7 it is 0.93·$LW_1$.
- In GUSTO-I (`ref_monotone_bound.csv`) the ratio $(W_1+|\Delta\mu|)/(2W_1)$ ranges over 0.66–1.00 for age and 0.72–1.00 for SBP. For the resolved partner R6 on age it is 0.60 vs 0.91 yr. For R1 and R5 on age it is about 0.68.

The attaining $\tau^*$ of l.181 has slope $\pm L$ that switches sign at every CDF crossing, which for S5 makes it V-shaped about 50. The manuscript itself says monotone direction is what published subgroup reports document (l.211, l.464).

(c) *Bounded effects.* The admissibility check at l.181 uses only $|\tau|\le1$. On a risk-difference scale the constraint is $-R_0(x)\le\tau(x)\le1-R_0(x)$. The attaining $\tau^*$ for age has range $L(b-a)=0.91$, so any admissible version needs treatment effects of up to about +89 points somewhere on the support. On a bounded support with $\{F_1\neq F_2\}$ the whole support, $\tau^*$ is unique up to a constant. With any realistic cap $|\tau|\le M<L(b-a)/2$, the supremum over the capped class is attained by some other function (Arzelà–Ascoli) and is therefore strictly below $LW_1$.

*Defensible claim.* "$LW_1$ is the sharp bound when the Lipschitz constant is the only restriction placed on the CATE (Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality). Further shape information tightens it: for a monotone CATE the sharp bound is $L(W_1+|\Delta\mu|)/2$, and a bounded-effect constraint tightens it further. $\Delta_{\max}$ is therefore a conservative upper bound." The reason to choose $W_1$ is then the per-unit-slope constant, plus the fact that the monotone bound is a simple function of $W_1$ and the mean difference. It is not "attainment". The authors may also want to report $(W_1+|\Delta\mu|)/2$ beside $W_1$. Whether to do so is theirs to decide.

### M2. The "11 of 13 cells" efficiency tally counts cells of a designed grid, and the one reversal has a general mechanism (tail weight) that the manuscript misreads
Abstract l.43; §4.1.3 l.304 ("the control removes $W_1$'s Set 4 advantage rather than reversing the order in general"); l.508; l.589 ("the reason to use $W_1$ … reaching the selection target with fewer patients than the KS statistic in 11 of 13 cells").

Thirteen cells are not a sample of configurations. They are a design: three cells favour $W_1$ by construction (acknowledged), six are light-tailed two-parameter families (Sets 1–2), three are mixtures with matched mean and SD, and one is a location shift on a heavy-tailed population. The one reversal (bulk shift: KS 333 [318, 348] vs $W_1$ 377 [362, 392]) is not specific to Set 4. For a shift without crossing, $\widehat W_1$ behaves like a mean difference, so its sampling SD scales with the total SD of the population. The Set 4 anchor has total SD $\sqrt{0.9\cdot100+0.1(64+1600)}=16.0$ against a bulk SD of 10. The KS statistic is rank-based, and its signal falls only by the bulk-density factor 0.9.

My check (`ref_tailweight.R` / `.log`; 1,500 replicates, shift 2.5, equal n) applies the same shift to the Gaussian family and to the Set 4 family:

| Family | n | AUC $W_1$ | AUC KS | SD of null $\widehat W_1$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | 200 | 0.913 | 0.884 | 0.443 |
| Set 4 | 200 | 0.811 | 0.839 | 0.721 |
| Gaussian | 400 | 0.986 | 0.975 | — |
| Set 4 | 400 | 0.932 | 0.948 | — |

So the order does reverse whenever a location difference sits on a population with heavy or contaminated tails. That is the "rare displaced subgroup" setting the Discussion (l.589) names as where the method matters. The Set 4 pooled error rates at l.320 (0.718 vs 0.971) pool four discordant types. Two of them, plus the prevalence type, favour $W_1$ by construction, and the sentence carries no such qualifier.

*Defensible claim.* "In the simulated cells, $W_1$ reached AUC 0.9 with fewer patients than the KS statistic in the light-tailed location/scale/shape cells and in the displaced-subgroup cells (the latter by construction). The KS statistic needed fewer in the heavy-tailed location-shift cell, because tail weight inflates the sampling variance of $\widehat W_1$ but not of the rank-based KS statistic." The abstract should either drop the tally or attach this qualifier.

### M4. Study 1, "the primary comparison", tests a different task from the one the application performs
§4 l.249 ("Study 1 carries the primary comparison"); §4.1.1 l.281; §5.3 l.508; Discussion l.589.

In the application no partner is identical: 9 of 15 age distances and 14 of 15 SBP distances are resolved against the null floor. The reported output is an ordering by magnitude among differing partners, read with rank intervals. Study 1 does not evaluate whether $\widehat W_1$ orders differing candidates correctly, and its AUC pools all discordant candidates against matches. The manuscript says (l.508) that Study 1's required sample sizes "do not transfer" to GUSTO-I, and justifies $W_1$ there by the bound (see M1). In the binding analysis below, therefore, Study 1 determines no statement in the application. A referee will ask either for an ordering metric (for example Kendall's τ between $\widehat W_1$ ranks and true-$W_1$ ranks among the discordant candidates, which the existing replicates could provide) or for a statement that the application's ordering is not supported by Study 1.

*Defensible claim.* "Study 1 shows that SMD cannot detect certain differences at any n, and that $W_1$ and KS can. It does not evaluate the ordering of non-identical partners, which is what the application reports."

### M5. Study 2's quoted coverage range does not include any distance in the application

9. **SBP digit preference.** The share of SBP readings on multiples of 10 ranges from 0.199 to 0.795 across regions (R8 0.795), and the absolute heaping difference correlates with $\widehat W_{1,\rm SBP}$ (Spearman 0.68). My check (`ref_heaping.R`/`.csv`) is reassuring: rounding all values to 10 mmHg, or de-heaping by uniform jitter, changes $\widehat W_{1,\rm SBP}$ by at most 0.2 mmHg, and the ranking keeps Spearman 0.986/0.989. This is worth one sentence, because heaping is visible in the data and step CDFs bear on Appendix l.673.
10. **Bootstrap Monte Carlo error at B = 2000 is not reported.** My rerun moves CI endpoints by up to 0.09 mmHg (SBP) and 0.05 yr (age), so 2-dp endpoints overstate their precision. The Table 9 note's "just above $L_{\rm UB}$" for R1 (0.00203 vs 0.002) would flip only if $W_U$ exceeded 5.00 (CSV 4.93; my rerun 4.90). It is robust, but say so.
11. **Data availability (l.647).** The code location is a placeholder ("[repository URL]"). The analysis reads `data/gusto.csv`, not `predtools::gusto` directly. Give the extraction step or a checksum. N = 40,830 matches.
12. **Selection measure.** l.281 gives every measure the oracle $k$. State in the abstract's simulation sentence that the efficiency comparison is "at AUC 0.9 on a selection task" (it does), and that $k$ is supplied (it does not).
