42: \abstract[Abstract]{%
43: The ICH E17 guideline describes regional pooling in multi-regional clinical trials (MRCTs) in terms of the similarity of effect modifier distributions but names no measure, and, to our knowledge, no distance between regional effect modifier distributions has been evaluated for this purpose. We quantify how two regions' distributions of each candidate effect modifier differ by the Wasserstein-1 ($W_1$) distance with bootstrap confidence intervals. If the conditional average treatment effect is common to all regions, depends on that modifier alone, and is Lipschitz with clinical slope $L_{\text{clinical}}$, regional average effects differ by at most $\Delta_{\max} = L_{\text{clinical}} W_1$; when $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is unknown, the smallest slope $L^*$ at which the distance could produce a clinical margin is compared with a sponsor's bound. In simulations, the standardized mean difference stayed at chance in some configurations up to 2,000 patients per region, whereas $W_1$ and the Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) statistic reached the discrimination target in every configuration; at that target (AUC 0.9), $W_1$ needed fewer patients than the KS statistic in \numval{nCellsW1FewerKS} of \numval{nCellsSelection} cells (three by construction), overlapped it in one, and needed more in a uniform-shift control. For true distances of about \numval{minW1SdS3toS7} to \numval{maxW1SdS3toS7} SD (normal reference, equal $n$), bootstrap coverage was \numval{covRangeS3toS7N100up2dpAbs} from 100 patients per region, below 0.95 beyond Monte Carlo error in \numval{nCellsCovBelow2MCSE} of ten cells. In a hypothetical GUSTO-I-based MRCT, partner rankings on age and on systolic blood pressure differed, and a null floor marked distances not distinguishable from no difference. The framework quantifies differences; the pooling judgment remains clinical.}

62: \section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
65: Multi-regional clinical trials (MRCTs), conducted across multiple countries or regulatory regions under a single protocol, have become the standard paradigm for global pharmaceutical development.\cite{chen2010,quan2010} The International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) E17 guideline, adopted in 2017, established principles for planning and designing MRCTs to evaluate whether treatment effects can be regarded as generalizable across the target population.\cite{iche17}
67: Regulatory authorities in Japan and China examine the consistency of treatment effects in regional subpopulations,\cite{matsushima2024,song2025} yet individual regional sample sizes in global MRCTs are often insufficient for such demonstration. A key strategy for addressing this challenge is the regional pooling approach, wherein regions with similar patient characteristics are grouped for analysis to enhance the ability to assess regional consistency.\cite{iche17} The ICH E17 guideline describes pooling decisions in terms of the similarity of effect modifier distributions.\cite{iche17}
69: An effect modifier is a baseline patient characteristic---such as age, disease severity, or genetic marker---for which the treatment benefit differs across subgroups. When such heterogeneity exists, even if the drug works identically at the individual level, regions with different patient compositions may observe different average treatment effects. A region with predominantly younger patients would show larger benefits than a region with predominantly older patients, not because the drug works differently, but because the patient mix differs. This fundamental relationship underscores why the similarity of the full effect modifier distribution, not only its average level, is critical to the validity of regional pooling. In this paper, we focus on continuous effect modifiers, such as age and disease severity scores; categorical effect modifiers raise distinct methodological considerations and are outside the present scope.
71: The extent to which the relevant effect modifiers are known varies across development programmes, and this shapes what can be planned quantitatively.\cite{komiyama2024} For some treatments, mechanism or prior trials identify a specific effect modifier in advance---a genetic marker known to determine response, for instance---so the characteristic requiring cross-regional comparison is established before planning. More often at the planning stage, the effect modifiers are only partially understood, and the sponsor proceeds from candidate characteristics suggested by biological plausibility or earlier-phase data, or from broader regional and ethnic factors that stand in as surrogates for the underlying modifiers.\cite{komiyama2024} The framework developed here operates on whichever set of candidate effect modifiers the sponsor specifies: it quantifies the cross-regional distributional similarity of each candidate, orders regions by it, and calibrates it clinically, and is agnostic to how the candidates were identified.
74: Despite the regulatory importance of effect modifier distributional similarity, current practice lacks a standardized, clinically calibrated methodology. The ICH E17 guideline provides no specific metric, threshold, or statistical procedure for determining when distributions are ``similar enough.'' Song et al., writing on the NMPA's implementation of ICH E17, identify the pooling strategy as an area where ambiguity remains and describe, when more than one effect modifier is considered, clustering populations on distances defined from the correlation between effect modifiers and endpoints, citing the reference volume discussed next.\cite{song2025} The pooling-strategy chapter of a reference volume on multi-regional trials under ICH E17 introduces, as one of many possible approaches, cluster analysis of regions on region-level summaries of the candidate effect modifiers: each region is given a representative value for each candidate effect modifier, the values are typically standardized, and distances such as the Euclidean distance between regions are weighted for relevance, for instance through a Lasso regression on the outcome; the chapter notes that the selection of the distance measure is one of the key decisions, and does not evaluate the approach.\cite{komiyama2024} Two gaps remain. First, to our knowledge no distance between regional effect modifier distributions has been proposed and evaluated for this purpose: a single summary per modifier leaves two regions that agree on it but differ in spread, shape, or tail behaviour at distance zero---for a binary characteristic, as in the chapter's illustration, one proportion describes the distribution completely, so the limitation concerns continuous modifiers---and any finite set of summaries requires deciding in advance which distributional features are relevant, rather than comparing the full distributions directly. Second, the chapter calls for a judgment of whether differences in the impact of effect modifiers across pooled regions are ``clinically meaningful or not'' but supplies no metric to operationalize that judgment.
77: These gaps bear on the planning stage, when the pooling strategy for small-sample regions is specified. In the PREVENT trial of secukinumab in non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis, a workshop report by Matsushima et al.\ describes a post hoc pooling in which countries with a similar distribution of effect modifiers were clustered into five pooled regions, with treatment differences that varied slightly with the distribution of CRP and MRI positivity and sex across them; the report attributes the inconsistent result in the Japanese subgroup to imbalanced treatment assignment and a small sample size rather than to effect modifiers.\cite{matsushima2024} Forming pooled regions from effect modifier distributions is the step that a distance between regional distributions would quantify. That case involved binary characteristics, where imbalance reduces to a difference in proportions; the continuous effect modifiers on which we focus can differ in shape and spread, not only average level, so assessing their similarity requires more than comparing means.
83: General-purpose comparison tools such as the standardized mean difference are routinely applied to baseline covariates,\cite{austin2011} but the standardized mean difference captures only differences in location: regions can match on means yet differ in variance or distributional shape. When effect modification is non-linear, these features directly influence regional average treatment effects.
89: This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a quantitative framework for assessing effect modifier distributional similarity across regions, based on the Wasserstein-1 ($W_1$) distance between two cumulative distribution functions, retained in the original measurement units of the effect modifier. Geometrically, $W_1$ equals the area between the two cumulative distribution functions. Unlike the standardized mean difference, $W_1$ responds to differences in variance and shape as well as location. We estimate $W_1$ with bootstrap confidence intervals and link it to clinical interpretation through a calibration framework.
97: The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:methods} presents the methodological framework: existing measures and their limitations, the definition of the per-effect-modifier $W_1$ distance, the estimation procedure, and its clinical calibration. Section~\ref{sec:simulation} presents two simulation studies, evaluating identification and estimation properties. Section~\ref{sec:application} illustrates the framework with publicly available individual patient data from the GUSTO-I trial. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications, limitations, and future directions.
100: \section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
103: An effect modifier is a factor for which the treatment effect varies across subgroups. Formally, let the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) be denoted $\tau(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]$, where $X$ is the effect modifier. When this function is non-constant, the average treatment effect observed in region $r$ depends on the distribution $F_r$ of the effect modifier in that region:
104: \begin{equation}
105: \bar{\tau}_r = \int \tau(x) \, dF_r(x).
106: \label{eq:regional_ate}
107: \end{equation}
108: Assessing pooling suitability therefore requires a measure of distributional difference between regions.
112: \subsection{Existing Approaches and Their Limitations}\label{sec:existing}
114: The most widely used measure for comparing covariate distributions across groups is the standardized mean difference (SMD), defined as
115: \begin{equation}
116: \text{SMD} = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{S_{\text{pooled}}},
117: \label{eq:smd}
118: \end{equation}
119: where $\bar{X}_r$ denotes the sample mean in region $r$ and $S_{\text{pooled}}$ is the pooled standard deviation.\cite{austin2011} Because treatment effects in clinical trials are typically summarized as mean differences, SMD provides a scale-free comparison directly relevant to the quantities that drive clinical decision-making. However, by construction SMD captures only differences in location. Two distributions with identical means but markedly different variances---for example, $N(50, 5^2)$ and $N(50, 15^2)$---yield $\text{SMD} = 0$ despite a threefold difference in spread.
121: Distributional distance measures address this limitation by comparing distributions beyond summary statistics. Empirical distribution function (EDF) tests such as the Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) statistic, $D_{\text{KS}} = \sup_x |F_1(x) - F_2(x)|$, and related measures (Cram\'{e}r--von Mises, Anderson--Darling) quantify differences in the full shape of distributions by comparing CDFs directly. Information-theoretic divergences such as Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence,
122: \begin{equation}
123: D_{\text{KL}}(P \| Q) = \int p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \, dx,
124: \label{eq:kl}
125: \end{equation}
126: take a density-based approach, measuring how one probability distribution differs from another.
130: However, each of these approaches has limitations when applied to comparing effect modifier distributions across regions. SMD captures only location differences and is blind to variance and shape. EDF-based statistics such as the KS statistic capture full distributional differences but lack a clinically interpretable scale, and the bound on treatment effect heterogeneity they admit is governed by the total variation of the CATE, a constant that is clinically stated only for a monotone CATE (Section~\ref{sec:nabcd_metric}). KL divergence is asymmetric---comparing region A to region B yields a different value than comparing B to A---can diverge to infinity when empirical distributions do not fully overlap, and requires density estimation that is impractical with small regional samples. None of these measures offers a bound on regional treatment effect differences that is both attained---the smallest valid bound over the Lipschitz class---and governed by a per-unit clinical slope, the kind of quantity that treatment-by-covariate interaction analyses estimate. These gaps motivate a measure that (i) captures distributional features beyond location, (ii) provides a clinically interpretable scale, and (iii) offers such a bound. The Wasserstein-1 distance and its clinical calibration, developed in the following subsections, satisfy all three requirements.
132: \subsection{Per-Effect-Modifier Wasserstein-1 Distance}\label{sec:nabcd_metric}
136: Among distributional distances that could capture the full distributional differences relevant to regional pooling, we adopt the Wasserstein-1 distance ($W_1$) because its heterogeneity bound (Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity} below) is attained within the class defined by the per-unit clinical slope $L_{\text{clinical}}$: among the bounds with that constant, it is the smallest valid one. The $W_1$ distance (sometimes called the Earth Mover's Distance) admits three equivalent representations on the real line, each highlighting a different facet of its interpretation.\cite{villani2009,vallender1974}
138: Let $F_1$ and $F_2$ denote the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of an effect modifier in two regions. Then
139: \begin{equation}
140: W_1(F_1, F_2) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |F_1(x) - F_2(x)| \, dx = \int_0^1 |F_1^{-1}(u) - F_2^{-1}(u)| \, du = \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(F_1, F_2)} \int |x - y| \, d\pi(x, y),
141: \label{eq:wasserstein}
142: \end{equation}
143: where $F_r^{-1}$ is the quantile function and $\Pi(F_1, F_2)$ is the set of probability couplings with marginals $F_1$ and $F_2$. The CDF form expresses $W_1$ as the area between the two CDFs (Figure~\ref{fig:nabcd_definition}); the quantile form as the integrated absolute difference of quantile functions; and the Kantorovich form as the minimum expected absolute displacement to transport one distribution onto the other. The three forms are mathematically equivalent.\cite{vallender1974} Unlike the standardized mean difference, $W_1$ responds to differences in variance, skewness, and other distributional features.\cite{panaretos2019}
145: \begin{figure}[t]
146: \centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{fig1_w1_definition.pdf}}
147: \caption{The Wasserstein-1 distance as the area between cumulative distribution functions, illustrated with a right-skewed Region~1 (gamma distribution) and a symmetric Region~2 (normal distribution). Panel~(A): probability densities. Panel~(B): cumulative distribution functions; the shaded area equals $W_1(F_1, F_2)$.\label{fig:nabcd_definition}}
148: \end{figure}
151: A property of $W_1$ central to our framework is that it carries the units of the underlying effect modifier. In each of the three equivalent forms in equation~(\ref{eq:wasserstein}), $W_1$ carries the units of $X$: the CDF form integrates a dimensionless difference of probabilities over $x$, the quantile form integrates differences of quantiles, and the coupling form integrates distances $|x - y|$. Consequently, $W_1(F_1, F_2)$ is expressed on the original scale of the effect modifier. This dimensional alignment supports direct clinical interpretation: a regional $W_1 = 5$~mmHg in systolic blood pressure means that optimally transporting one distribution onto the other moves probability mass an average distance of 5~mmHg. The same logic precludes a meaningful direct comparison of $W_1$ values across effect modifiers measured on different scales, and we therefore restrict attention in this paper to per-effect-modifier assessment (Section~\ref{sec:discussion} elaborates on this scope choice).
153: $W_1$ is a proper metric on the space of probability distributions with finite first moment, satisfying non-negativity, symmetry, identity of indiscernibles, and the triangle inequality.\cite{villani2009} It is also affine-equivariant in the variable units, satisfying $W_1(F_{aX+b}, F_{aY+b}) = |a| \cdot W_1(F_X, F_Y)$ for any $a \neq 0$. Beyond these basic properties, $W_1$ admits a Kantorovich--Rubinstein dual representation\cite{villani2009}
154: \begin{equation}
155: W_1(F_1, F_2) = \sup_{\|f\|_{\text{Lip}} \leq 1} \left|\int f \, dF_1 - \int f \, dF_2\right|,
156: \label{eq:dual}
157: \end{equation}
158: which expresses $W_1$ as the largest possible difference in expected values of a 1-Lipschitz function $f$ between the two distributions (the notation $\|f\|_{\text{Lip}} \leq 1$ means $|f(x) - f(y)| \leq |x - y|$ for all $x, y$). If the conditional average treatment effect function $\tau(x)$ is Lipschitz continuous with constant $L_{\text{clinical}}$, so that $|\tau(x) - \tau(y)| \leq L_{\text{clinical}} |x - y|$ for all $x, y$, then $\tau / L_{\text{clinical}}$ is 1-Lipschitz and applying equation~(\ref{eq:dual}) yields the central result of this section:
161: \begin{proposition}\label{prop:heterogeneity}
162: \textup{(Connection to heterogeneity).} Suppose the CATE function $\tau$ is the same in every region and depends on the effect modifier $X$ alone. If $\tau$ is Lipschitz continuous with constant $L_{\text{clinical}}$, then for any two regions with effect modifier distributions $F_1$ and $F_2$,
163: \begin{equation}
164: |\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2| \leq L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot W_1(F_1, F_2).
165: \label{eq:heterogeneity_bound}
166: \end{equation}
167: \end{proposition}
169: \begin{proof}
170: Apply equation~(\ref{eq:dual}) with $f = \tau / L_{\text{clinical}}$, which is 1-Lipschitz by assumption. This yields $\left|\int (\tau / L_{\text{clinical}}) \, d(F_1 - F_2)\right| \leq W_1(F_1, F_2)$. Combining with equation~(\ref{eq:regional_ate}) for $\bar{\tau}_r$ and rearranging yields equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}).
171: \end{proof}
181: \noindent Equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) is not the only bound of its form. Integration by parts gives $\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2 = -\int (F_1(x) - F_2(x)) \, d\tau(x)$, so for a CATE of bounded variation on a bounded support, $|\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2| \leq \mathrm{TV}(\tau) \cdot D_{\text{KS}}$ as well, and $W_1 \leq W_2$ makes $L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot W_2$ a valid but looser bound.\cite{villani2009} Two properties distinguish equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}), and they act together. The first is attainment within the Lipschitz class. For $\tau^*(x) = L_{\text{clinical}} \int_{x_0}^{x} \operatorname{sgn}(F_2(t) - F_1(t))\,dt$, with $x_0$ the midpoint of the support (an additive constant does not change $\bar{\tau}^*_1 - \bar{\tau}^*_2$), which is $L_{\text{clinical}}$-Lipschitz, $\bar{\tau}^*_1 - \bar{\tau}^*_2 = L_{\text{clinical}} \int |F_1 - F_2|\,dx = L_{\text{clinical}}\,W_1$, so the bound is the supremum of $|\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2|$ over all common $L_{\text{clinical}}$-Lipschitz CATEs (the Kantorovich--Rubinstein duality of equation~\ref{eq:dual}): every valid bound with the same constant, including $L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot W_2$ and, on a support $[a, b]$, $L_{\text{clinical}} (b - a) D_{\text{KS}}$, is at least as large, and the $L^*$ of Section~\ref{sec:inference} is exactly the smallest slope at which some such CATE produces the margin. On a risk-difference scale $|\tau| \leq 1$, so $\tau^*$ is admissible when $L_{\text{clinical}} (b - a) \leq 2$; for the bounds and supports of Section~\ref{sec:application} the products are \numval{lipRangeAge} (age) and \numval{lipRangeSbp} (SBP). The Kolmogorov--Smirnov bound is likewise attained within its own class, by a step CATE of height $\mathrm{TV}(\tau)$ at the point where $|F_1 - F_2|$ is largest; what differs between the two bounds is the class, and hence the constant. The second is the constant: $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is a change in treatment effect per unit of the effect modifier, the kind of quantity that treatment-by-covariate interaction analyses estimate, whereas $\mathrm{TV}(\tau)$ is the total excursion of the CATE over the support, which reduces to a clinically stated quantity only for a monotone CATE. Where both constants are available, neither bound dominates in general. On a support $[a, b]$, $W_1 \leq (b - a)\,D_{\text{KS}}$ and $\mathrm{TV}(\tau) \leq L_{\text{clinical}}\,(b - a)$, and $L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot W_1 \leq \mathrm{TV}(\tau) \cdot D_{\text{KS}}$ holds exactly when $W_1 / D_{\text{KS}} \leq \mathrm{TV}(\tau) / L_{\text{clinical}}$. For a CATE whose slope has constant magnitude $L_{\text{clinical}}$, such as a linear CATE, $\mathrm{TV}(\tau) = L_{\text{clinical}}(b - a)$ and equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) is never the looser of the two; the Kolmogorov--Smirnov bound gains as the CATE's slope falls below its maximum over much of the support, most sharply when the CATE changes steeply over a narrow range---the threshold case in which equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) is itself conservative (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
183: The right-hand side of equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) involves $W_1$ in its original measurement units, multiplied by a clinical slope $L_{\text{clinical}}$; no further normalization of $W_1$ is required to obtain a clinically interpretable bound. We therefore adopt $W_1$ in its original units as the primary similarity metric throughout this paper. One could instead normalize $W_1$ by a within-effect-modifier scale measure such as the pooled interquartile range; we show in Section~\ref{sec:inference} that such normalization is mathematically redundant once equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) is used for clinical calibration. The Lipschitz assumption underlying Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity} is appropriate when the CATE function varies smoothly with the effect modifier; for threshold or step-function CATEs, $L_{\text{clinical}}$ can be arbitrarily large and the bound becomes conservative (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
185: \subsection{Estimation}\label{sec:estimation}
189: Given samples $\{X_{1,i}\}_{i=1}^{n_1}$ from region 1 and $\{X_{2,j}\}_{j=1}^{n_2}$ from region 2, the Wasserstein-1 distance is estimated using empirical distribution functions. Since $\hat{F}_1$ and $\hat{F}_2$ are piecewise constant (right-continuous step functions), the integral in equation~(\ref{eq:wasserstein}) reduces to a finite sum over the intervals between consecutive distinct values of the pooled sample:
190: \begin{equation}
191: \widehat{W}_1 = \sum_{k=1}^{m-1} |\hat{F}_1(x_{(k)}) - \hat{F}_2(x_{(k)})| \cdot (x_{(k+1)} - x_{(k)}),
192: \label{eq:estimator}
193: \end{equation}
195: where $\hat{F}_r$ denotes the empirical CDF (right-continuous) of region $r$ and $x_{(1)} < \cdots < x_{(m)}$ are the $m$ distinct values of the pooled sample (ties are pervasive when an effect modifier is recorded on a coarse grid, as with systolic blood pressure recorded in whole mmHg in Section~\ref{sec:application}). The estimator $\widehat{W}_1$ inherits the original measurement units of the effect modifier. The asymptotic distribution of $W_1$ is non-standard: del Barrio et al.\cite{delbarrio1999} showed that, when $\int \sqrt{F(1-F)}\,dx < \infty$, $\sqrt{n}\,W_1(\hat{F}_n, F) \xrightarrow{d} \int |B(F(x))|\,dx$, where $B$ is a standard Brownian bridge. Because the quantiles of this limiting distribution depend on the unknown $F$, no universal critical values are available. In the two-sample case a Gaussian limit, and hence a Wald interval, exists under the measure-zero condition stated below, but the condition cannot be verified from data and the limit's variance involves $\operatorname{sgn}(F_1 - F_2)$, which is unstable where the CDFs cross. We therefore employ the nonparametric percentile bootstrap with $B = 2{,}000$ replicates.\cite{efron1993} Each bootstrap replicate draws $n_1$ observations with replacement from region 1 and, independently, $n_2$ from region 2, and recomputes $\widehat{W}_1$ by equation~(\ref{eq:estimator}); the 95\% interval is formed by the 2.5th and 97.5th sample percentiles of the $B$ replicates (R's default quantile definition, type 7). In the two-sample setting, when $\int \sqrt{F_r(1 - F_r)}\,dx < \infty$ for $r = 1, 2$, $n_1/(n_1 + n_2)$ stays away from 0 and 1, and the set $\{x : F_1(x) = F_2(x) \in (0,1)\}$ has Lebesgue measure zero---for example, when the two CDFs cross only at isolated points---the Hadamard derivative of the $L_1$ functional at $F_1 - F_2$ is linear (applying the results of Fang and Santos to this functional), and the ordinary nonparametric bootstrap is consistent by the functional delta method.\cite{fang2019} Where the CDFs coincide on an interval, including $F_1 = F_2$, the derivative is non-linear and the naive bootstrap is not consistent.\cite{dumbgen1993,fang2019,sommerfeld2018} Convergence occurs at rate $\sqrt{n_1 n_2/(n_1+n_2)}$ (see Appendix~\ref{app:asymptotic} for details). The estimator~(\ref{eq:estimator}) is computed in $O((n_1 + n_2) \log(n_1 + n_2))$ time, dominated by sorting the combined order statistics.
199: Because $W_1$ is a distance, $\widehat{W}_1$ is non-negative by construction and is typically positive even when the two regions are draws from the same distribution. We refer to the sampling distribution of $\widehat{W}_1$ under $F_1 = F_2$ as the null floor. It is obtained by drawing two independent resamples, of the two regions' actual sizes, from one region's empirical distribution---the anchor's in Section~\ref{sec:app_operability}---and recomputing $\widehat{W}_1$; because it depends on both sample sizes and on the source distribution, it is computed per region pair. An observed $\widehat{W}_1$ at or below its 95th percentile, the reference point used in Section~\ref{sec:app_operability}, is one the estimator would produce with appreciable probability from identical distributions, so it is not evidence of a difference; how large the difference could be is read from the upper confidence limit of $\widehat{W}_1$, equivalently of $\Delta_{\max}$. Declaring a distance unresolved in this way is a one-sided test of $F_1 = F_2$ at level 0.05; it is used only to mark which estimates are read at their upper limit.
201: \subsection{Interpretation and Clinical Calibration}\label{sec:inference}
203: A central feature of $W_1$ is its connection to treatment effect heterogeneity through Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity}. This connection provides the basis for a clinically grounded interpretation framework rather than reliance on fixed numerical cutoffs.
211: We refer to the Lipschitz constant of the CATE function $\tau(x)$ as the clinical slope $L_{\text{clinical}}$. By definition, $L_{\text{clinical}}$ quantifies the maximum change in the treatment effect per unit change in the effect modifier, expressed on the outcome scale of the trial---for example, absolute 30-day mortality risk per year of age, or per mmHg of systolic blood pressure. Throughout this paper $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is quoted on the proportion scale of the outcome, so that $L_{\text{clinical}} = 1 \times 10^{-2}$ per year denotes one percentage point of absolute 30-day mortality per year of age; clinical margins $\Delta_{\text{clin}}$ stated in percentage points are divided by 100 before entering any calculation. When prior individual patient data (IPD) are available, meta-regression on treatment-by-covariate interaction estimates the average interaction slope.\cite{riley2010,fisher2017} An average slope is, in absolute value, a lower bound on the Lipschitz constant rather than the constant itself: for non-linear $\tau$, the local slope can exceed the fitted one. An $L_{\text{clinical}}$ specified from such an estimate should therefore carry a sponsor-specified multiplicative safety factor, in the spirit of margin discounting in non-inferiority design, so that the premise of Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity} remains defensible. More commonly at the MRCT planning stage, published subgroup reports document the direction of interaction rather than a per-unit slope, and a precise numerical $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is unavailable. We address both situations below.
213: From Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity}, the maximum potential difference in regional average treatment effects attributable to effect modifier distributional differences is
214: \begin{equation}
215: \Delta_{\max} = L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot W_1(F_1, F_2),
216: \label{eq:delta_max}
217: \end{equation}
218: expressed in the units of the trial outcome scale. The formula is invariant to within-effect-modifier scale normalization of $W_1$: any normalized form $W_1 / X$ multiplied by the same scale $X$ cancels in equation~(\ref{eq:delta_max}), so adopting $W_1$ in its original units yields the same clinical-scale calibration as any rescaled version. When $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is independently known, equation~(\ref{eq:delta_max}) provides a direct clinical-scale translation of the observed distributional difference. When $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is unknown, the relation can be inverted to ask which $L_{\text{clinical}}$ would be required for a given $W_1$ to produce a clinically meaningful $\Delta_{\max}$.
221: The estimator $\widehat{W}_1$ has a percentile bootstrap CI (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}), and inferential uncertainty propagates linearly to $\Delta_{\max}$ when $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is treated as fixed: $[\Delta_{\max,L}, \Delta_{\max,U}] = L_{\text{clinical}} \cdot [W_{1,L}, W_{1,U}]$. When $L_{\text{clinical}}$ itself is uncertain or unavailable, we solve equation~(\ref{eq:delta_max}) for $L_{\text{clinical}}$ at a pre-specified clinical margin $\Delta_{\text{clin}}$ (the minimal clinically important treatment effect difference, the non-inferiority margin, or the targeted overall effect), yielding the required clinical slope
222: \begin{equation}
223: L^* = \frac{\Delta_{\text{clin}}}{W_1(F_1, F_2)}.
224: \label{eq:lstar}
225: \end{equation}
226: $L^*$ is the smallest clinical slope under which the observed distributional difference could produce a clinically meaningful regional treatment effect difference. Because $L^*$ is a decreasing function of $W_1$, the bootstrap CI of $\widehat{W}_1$ gives the percentile interval $[\Delta_{\text{clin}}/W_{1,U},\, \Delta_{\text{clin}}/W_{1,L}]$ for $L^*$. If a plausible upper bound on $L_{\text{clinical}}$ from prior evidence (denoted $L_{\text{UB}}$) lies below $L^*$, the distributional difference cannot produce such a difference, provided $L_{\text{clinical}} \leq L_{\text{UB}}$; reading $L_{\text{UB}}$ against the lower limit $\Delta_{\text{clin}}/W_{1,U}$ rather than the point value carries the estimation uncertainty of $\widehat{W}_1$ into this comparison. The two pathways---forward calibration via equation~(\ref{eq:delta_max}) when $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is known and reverse comparison via equation~(\ref{eq:lstar}) against $L_{\text{UB}}$ when $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is unknown---cover the two evidence states encountered at the MRCT planning stage.
235: Specifying $L_{\text{clinical}}$ (or its upper bound $L_{\text{UB}}$) is a clinical judgment, informed but not determined by prior evidence, for which the sponsor carries responsibility---the same footing as a non-inferiority margin. Writing $R_a(x)$ for the risk under arm $a$, $\tau(x) = R_1(x) - R_0(x)$, so $|\tau'(x)| \leq |R_1'(x)| + |R_0'(x)|$: arm-level risk gradients constrain the modification slope only jointly, and a prognostic gradient alone guarantees no bound, prognostic and treatment-effect gradients being distinct quantities.\cite{ballman2015,kent2020} For the GUSTO-I case study presented in Section~\ref{sec:application}, the Fibrinolytic Therapy Trialists' meta-analysis\cite{ftt1994}, GUSTO-I subgroup analyses,\cite{gusto1993} and the GUSTO-I prognostic model\cite{lee1995} inform illustrative bounds of $L_{\text{UB,age}} \approx 1 \times 10^{-2}$ per year and $L_{\text{UB,SBP}} \approx 2 \times 10^{-3}$ per mmHg on the 30-day mortality scale; these exceed the empirical prognostic age gradient of approximately $6 \times 10^{-3}$ per year derived from the age-stratified mortality reported by Lee et al.,\cite{lee1995} though not the sum of two arm-level gradients of that size that the inequality above permits---a reference point rather than a guarantee. Conclusions drawn from the bounds are conditional on them; because $L^*$ is reported for every region pair, any alternative bound can be compared against it directly. Alternative specification strategies include the deft estimator of Fisher et al.\cite{fisher2017}
240: The pooling assessment proceeds by computing per-effect-modifier $W_1$ with bootstrap CIs across candidate partner regions, ordering the candidates by the point estimate $\widehat{W}_1$ within each effect modifier, reading each distance against its null floor and its upper confidence limit (Section~\ref{sec:estimation})---distances unresolved against the null floor are not ordered among themselves---and deriving $\Delta_{\max}$ when $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is known or $L^*$ at a pre-specified $\Delta_{\text{clin}}$ when $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is unknown. The framework is deliberately estimation-centered rather than testing-based: $W_1$ has no universal cutoff that separates ``similar'' from ``dissimilar,'' and what a given $W_1$ means clinically depends on the clinical slope $L_{\text{clinical}}$, which is external to the distributional comparison. By presenting per-pair $\Delta_{\max}$ (or $L^*$) with bootstrap CIs alongside the clinical context---the overall treatment effect size, the non-inferiority margin, the minimal clinically important difference---the framework provides quantitative inputs for sponsor and regulatory judgment. The judgment is not driven by a significance test.\cite{wasserstein2016} The one place a test enters is the null floor, a one-sided test of $F_1 = F_2$ at level 0.05 (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}); it governs how an estimate is displayed and read, not whether regions are pooled.
243: \section{Simulation Studies}\label{sec:simulation}
249: We conducted two complementary simulation studies. Study 1 evaluates identification: whether each candidate distance measure correctly identifies the candidate regions built from the anchor's population, whose effect modifier distributions are identical to the anchor's, by ranking candidate partners for an anchor region. Study 2 evaluates estimation performance: bias, variability, and coverage probability of bootstrap confidence intervals for $\widehat{W}_1$. Study 1 carries the primary comparison, because a distance that cannot distinguish differing distributions cannot usefully be calibrated; Study 2 underwrites the calibration pathway that converts an estimated distance into a $\Delta_{\max}$ statement via equation~(\ref{eq:delta_max}).
253: Because the two studies answer different questions, they produce different sample-size statements. Study 2 describes estimation over $n = 50$--200 per region: from $n = 100$, for the non-null scenarios (true distances of about \numval{minW1SdS3toS7} to \numval{maxW1SdS3toS7} SD of the reference distribution, equal $n$), bias was small relative to $W_1$ and coverage was \numval{covRangeS3toS7N100up2dp}, below 0.95 beyond Monte Carlo error in \numval{nCellsCovBelow2MCSE} of ten cells (Section~\ref{sec:sim_results}). Study 1's required sample sizes are discrimination statements: the sample size at which a measure's discrimination reaches an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of at least 0.9, which for $\widehat{W}_1$ ranges from \numval{reqnW1minSets34} to \numval{reqnW1maxSets34} per region across the cells of Sets 3 and 4. The two statements are labelled throughout and are not interchangeable.
255: \subsection{Study 1: Identification}\label{sec:study1}
267: \subsubsection{Design}\label{sec:study1_design}
272: Study 1 follows the ADEMP structure (aims, data-generating mechanisms, estimands, methods, performance measures) for simulation studies.\cite{morris2019} Four worlds are simulated, each built from a single distributional family and each containing regions constructed either from the anchor's own population (true matches) or from a discordant population. Set 1 (Gaussian) places discordance in location and scale. Set 2 (log-normal) places it in location and in dispersion combined with shape. Set 3 (mixture) is the adversarial world for summary-based measures: every region, matched or discordant, is constructed with mean 50 and SD 10, so all discordance lies in distributional shape. Set 4 (extremes) places a 5\% subgroup in each tail, 40 units from the centre in the anchor; discordant regions move both subgroups to 70 or 100 units or raise their prevalence to 12\% (mean fixed), or move only the upper subgroup to 100 units, the pattern of a small severe subpopulation; two control regions instead shift their whole population by 2 and 3 units, a configuration without a displaced subgroup. In the Set 4 subgroup cells the two full-distribution measures respond differently by construction: a subgroup of prevalence $\varepsilon$ displaced by $d$ changes the KS statistic by at most about $\varepsilon$ whatever $d$ is, while it changes $W_1$ by $\varepsilon d$, so $W_1$'s sample-size advantage in those cells follows from the mechanism, and the bulk-shift controls are the cells that remove it. Ground truth is the construction label---which regions were built from the anchor's population---so the target is defined without reference to any of the compared measures and the comparison is not circular.
277: Three measures enter the comparison: $W_1$; the KS statistic, the most common comparison of full distributions; and SMD, the standard comparison of location. On the log-normal world, SMD computed on the log scale is also run; the transformation recovers the shape contrast (AUC \numval{aucSMDlogShapeN2000} at $n = 2{,}000$) but leaves the combined cell at chance (\numval{aucSMDlogCombinedN2000}), so a transformation that suits one contrast does not repair another. Under the registered protocol the simulation also evaluated three representative-value distances built from one to three regional moments, a harm measure based on the largest $W_1$ among the retained regions, and a clustering task with the number of groups estimated by the silhouette; these are not reported here because the paper compares distribution-level measures with the single-summary SMD and prescribes no grouping procedure, and their full outputs are in the repository (Data availability statement).
281: The measures are scored against the construction label on a selection task. An anchor and nine candidate regions are drawn; each measure ranks the candidates by their distance to the anchor and retains the $k$ closest, where $k$ is the number of true matches. Giving every measure the same $k$ removes threshold choice from the comparison entirely; supplying $k$ is itself an oracle, and the application chooses no $k$ but reads the ordering with the clinical calibration (Section~\ref{sec:application}). Performance is summarized by the AUC for discriminating true matches from discordant candidates (chance = 0.5) and by the probability that the $k$ retained regions include at least one discordant region. The task ran 10{,}000 replications per cell at the base grid ($n = 25$--100 per region) and 3{,}000 at the extended grid ($n = 150$--2{,}000). The extended grid, the accuracy target and the definitions of the two behaviours below were fixed after the base-grid results had been seen, and the Monte Carlo intervals of Table~\ref{tab:study2_required_n_mc} were added later; the reported target is the middle of the three examined.
283: \subsubsection{Two Behaviours: Blind and Recovering}\label{sec:study1_classification}
287: Extending the sample-size grid to $n = 2{,}000$ separates the measures into two behaviours (Figure~\ref{fig:study2_auc}). A measure is blind in a cell when its AUC stays at chance through $n = 2{,}000$: the population value of its distance carries no information about the construction label, so the task is unsolvable for that measure at any sample size. This is an identification failure, not a power failure. It is exact by construction in every mean-matched cell: the population SMD is zero there, whatever the sample size. On the selection task, SMD is blind in \numval{nCellsSMDblind} of the \numval{nCellsSelection} cells, counting each world's combined cell: every cell of Set 3, the two mean-matched cells of Set 4, the scale cell of Set 1, and the shape cell of Set 2 (AUC \numval{aucBlindSMDmin}--\numval{aucBlindSMDmax} across the full grid in all of them). Adding the SD as a further summary would separate the discordant regions in the four SMD-blind cells where they differ in dispersion, but not in Set 3, where every region shares mean and SD, nor would adding skewness in the shape-symmetric cell, where it is matched as well: any finite set of summaries can be matched by distributions that differ elsewhere.
290: A measure is recovering when its AUC rises monotonically through the target: identification succeeds, and what remains is a required-sample-size question. $W_1$ and the KS statistic are recovering in every cell. In particular, the KS statistic is blind nowhere: in the Set 4 cell where it is weakest, asymmetric severity, it still reaches selection AUC \numval{aucKSSet4asymsevN2000} at $n = 2{,}000$ (\numval{aucKSSet4symsevN2000} in the symmetric-severity cell). Its failures in what follows are failures of efficiency, not of identification.

455: \section{Application: Hypothetical Thrombolytic MRCT Using GUSTO-I Data}\label{sec:application}
459: We illustrate the per-effect-modifier Wasserstein-1 framework through a hypothetical scenario grounded in publicly available data from GUSTO-I, a large international trial in acute myocardial infarction (AMI). A sponsor is developing a novel thrombolytic agent (Drug~T) for AMI and is planning a Phase~3 MRCT across multiple regions. At the planning stage, the sponsor uses IPD from GUSTO-I ($N = \numval{gustoN}$; 16 regions)\cite{gusto1993} to quantify how the distributions of candidate effect modifiers in other regions differ from those in a designated anchor region, treating the GUSTO-I regions as stand-ins for the regional populations the new trial would enrol. GUSTO-I was not designed as an MRCT and its regions are anonymized.
461: \subsection{Scenario Design and Effect Modifier Selection}\label{sec:app_scenario}
464: For this illustrative exercise, the sponsor identifies two candidate effect modifiers for Drug~T's treatment effect in AMI: age and systolic blood pressure (SBP). Drug~T's Phase~2 programme has suggested age and SBP as candidate effect modifiers on the basis of exploratory analyses and biological plausibility, but the precision of these analyses is insufficient for numerical estimation of $L_{\text{clinical}}$. Class-level indirect evidence can at most inform a sponsor-specified upper bound (Section~\ref{sec:app_clinical}): the Fibrinolytic Therapy Trialists' (FTT) collaborative meta-analysis of existing fibrinolytic agents\cite{ftt1994} concluded that, among patients presenting with ST elevation or bundle-branch block, benefit is present irrespective of age and of blood pressure, and reports no per-unit CATE slope on the absolute mortality scale for either, so class transferability would not yield a defensible numerical prior for Drug~T.
466: We therefore treat $L_{\text{clinical}}$ as unknown a priori for both candidate effect modifiers, and apply the $L^*$ reverse-calculation pathway (equation~\ref{eq:lstar}) to both.

481: \subsection{Null Floor: Which Distances Are Distinguishable from No Difference?}\label{sec:app_operability}
485: Before interpreting the distances reported in Section~\ref{sec:app_nabcd}, we computed the null floor of Section~\ref{sec:estimation} for each candidate partner. 562: \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
570: This paper answers the requirements set out in Section~\ref{sec:existing} and characterizes the estimation of its answer. The $W_1$ distance meets requirement (i): it resolves distributional differences to which a location summary such as the SMD is structurally blind---in Study 1, SMD is at chance at every sample size examined in \numval{nCellsSMDblind} of the \numval{nCellsSelection} selection cells, an identification failure that no sample size repairs, whereas $W_1$ reaches the discrimination target in all four worlds, at $n = \numval{reqnW1minSets34}$--\numval{reqnW1maxSets34} per region in Sets 3 and 4 (Section~\ref{sec:study1_classification}). It meets requirements (ii) and (iii): the heterogeneity bound \(\Delta_{\max}\) (equation~\ref{eq:delta_max}) translates distributional distance into a clinically interpretable scale, is attained within the Lipschitz class and is therefore the smallest valid bound with its constant (Section~\ref{sec:nabcd_metric}), and that constant---a treatment effect change per unit of the effect modifier---is a clinical slope that the sponsor specifies, informed by clinical evidence. The corresponding Kolmogorov--Smirnov bound carries a total-variation constant instead, and SMD supports no bound valid across the Lipschitz class, since regions with equal means can have $W_1 > 0$ and hence, by equation~(\ref{eq:dual}), regional treatment effects differing by up to $L_{\text{clinical}} W_1$. Its estimation is characterized by Study~2: percentile bootstrap intervals whose coverage was \numval{covRangeS3toS7N100up2dp} for true distances of about \numval{minW1SdS3toS7} to \numval{maxW1SdS3toS7} SD in the location, scale, and combined scale-and-skewness scenarios at \(n \geq 100\) per region, below 0.95 beyond Monte Carlo error in \numval{nCellsCovBelow2MCSE} of the ten cells, with a characterized boundary behavior near the null (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}).
573: These methodological findings carry two interpretive implications for pooling assessments, illustrated by the GUSTO-I application as a worked example of the framework's operation rather than a substantive epidemiological claim. First, when multiple candidate effect modifiers are under consideration, the per-modifier distances need not agree: a partner close to the anchor on one effect modifier can be distant on another, as exemplified by the contrast between R2 and R9, so the distance on a modifier left out of the assessment need not be inferable from those included. Second, $W_1$ ranking alone does not convey clinical weight. Within one effect modifier, $L^* = \Delta_{\text{clin}} / W_1$ is a strictly decreasing function of $W_1$, so calibration does not reorder partners relative to their distances; what it adds is the comparison of each partner's $L^*$ with the bound $L_{\text{UB}}$ specified for that effect modifier, which places a distance measured in the modifier's own units on the outcome scale. The distance and its calibration therefore carry different information, and both are reported for each candidate effect modifier.
578: These implications also bear on the cluster analysis of regions that Komiyama et al.\ introduce as one example of forming pooled regions, in which the candidate effect modifiers are combined into a single weighted distance;\cite{komiyama2024} the clustering task run under our protocol, with the number of groups estimated, is not reported here (Section~\ref{sec:study1_design}). The per-effect-modifier resolution adopted here matters precisely because partner rankings can reverse across modifiers: in the GUSTO-I example R2 and R9 are each among the closest partners on one modifier and among the most distant on the other. The reversal is not confined to these two regions: across the 15 partners of Table~\ref{tab:gusto_nabcd}, the orderings by age and by systolic blood pressure distance do not agree (Spearman correlation $\numval{spearmanAgeVsSbpSigned}$; 95\% interval \numval{spearmanAgeVsSbpBootLo} to \numval{spearmanAgeVsSbpBootHi} from 1{,}000 patient-level bootstrap replicates that resample within each region and recompute both orderings; the correlation is computed on the point ordering of all 15 partners, including the distances that the display leaves unordered against their null floors, and the interval reflects sampling uncertainty within this trial);
580: the interval excludes the strong positive agreement under which one modifier's distances could stand in for the other's, so a single distance aggregated over the two modifiers could mask differences that a per-modifier assessment retains. How many pooled regions to form, and how orderings that differ across modifiers are reconciled, remain clinical judgments.
589: Of the limitations set out in Section~\ref{sec:existing}, the location-only limitation of SMD is structural, and Study 1 exhibits its consequence: in the mean-matched cells, SMD is at chance at every sample size examined while $W_1$ recovers the partner structure (Section~\ref{sec:study1_classification}); the lack of a clinical scale is addressed by the heterogeneity bound (equation~\ref{eq:delta_max}); and the limitations of KL divergence are avoided by construction, $W_1$ being symmetric, finite for any pair of empirical distributions, and computable from CDFs without density estimation. Addressing these limitations does not, however, mean returning a different answer in every dataset. In GUSTO-I, whose regional differences are predominantly differences in location (partner standard deviations within a factor of \numval{sdRatioMax} of the anchor's), $W_1$ ordered the 15 partners much as the SMD did (Spearman correlation \numval{spearmanW1SMDage}, 95\% interval \numval{spearmanW1SMDageLo} to \numval{spearmanW1SMDageHi}, for age and \numval{spearmanW1SMDsbp}, \numval{spearmanW1SMDsbpLo} to \numval{spearmanW1SMDsbpHi}, for systolic blood pressure, from the patient-level bootstrap of Table~\ref{tab:gusto_nabcd}), and its contribution there was the clinical calibration rather than a different answer; a survey of \numval{surveyPairs} region pairs in GUSTO-I,\cite{gusto1993} IST-1\cite{ist1_2000} and IST-3,\cite{ist3_2012} registered after an exploratory scan of the same data had shown the pattern (protocol and outputs in the repository), found agreement of the same order, with rank correlations between $W_1$ and $|\mathrm{SMD}|$ (pooled SD of the simple-average form) of \numval{surveySpearmanMin}--\numval{surveySpearmanMax} across \numval{surveyRows} trial-by-modifier combinations, five of them in GUSTO-I. The limitations bind where distributional differences go beyond location---mixtures and rare displaced subgroups, configurations that cannot be excluded in advance---and which kind of dataset one holds is not knowable before the distances are computed. The reason to use $W_1$ is therefore not that it always gives a different answer, but that, like the KS statistic, it recovers in every world, while reaching the selection target with fewer patients than the KS statistic in \numval{nCellsW1FewerKS} of \numval{nCellsSelection} cells at AUC 0.9, three of them by construction (Table~\ref{tab:study2_required_n_mc}), and carrying a translation to the clinical scale that is attained and whose constant is the per-unit clinical slope. It is not uniformly the more efficient: the intervals overlap in the shape-symmetric mixture, where the order depends on the target, the KS statistic needs fewer patients in the bulk-shift control, and in the Set 4 subgroup cells $W_1$'s advantage follows from the construction (Section~\ref{sec:study1_design}).
597: The distributional assessment and its calibration are separable: the identification and efficiency results of the preceding paragraph are properties of the distance itself and hold whether or not $L_{\text{clinical}}$ is available; clinical calibration adds an outcome-scale reading but does not change them.
602: Because $W_1$, like other covariate-distribution measures, requires only baseline effect modifier distributions, regional data can be assembled from prior trials, registries, electronic health records, or other real-world evidence (RWE) sources, provided the source population approximates the population each region is expected to enrol. The assessment can therefore be made at the planning stage, before any regional data from the new trial exist.
606: The $W_1$ distance is one input within a holistic regulatory evaluation; biological plausibility, clinical relevance, and benefit--risk balance extend beyond what any single distributional measure can quantify.\cite{iche17,matsushima2024} The framework has further limitations. It treats continuous effect modifiers marginally, leaving categorical and multivariate extensions for future work. Because $W_1$ is non-negative, $\widehat{W}_1$ exhibits positive bias near zero, zero percentile coverage when the true value is zero (S1), and undercoverage for small distances (S2: \numval{covS2n50disc} and S5: \numval{covS5n50disc} at $n = 50$), with the intervals erring above the true value (Section~\ref{sec:sim_results});\cite{andrews2000} estimates near the null are read against the null floor, and the coverage reported for Study~2 from $n = 100$ per region refers to true distances of about \numval{minW1SdS3toS7} to \numval{maxW1SdS3toS7} SD of the reference distribution, with equal regional sizes. Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity} also assumes a CATE common to all regions; regional differences in extrinsic factors that alter $\tau$ itself, such as standard of care or concomitant therapy, lie outside what $W_1$ measures. Finally, as with any covariate-based approach, unmeasured effect modifiers may contribute to heterogeneity beyond what the distance captures.
616: The calibration rests on the Lipschitz premise of Proposition~\ref{prop:heterogeneity}, and its limitations are those of the premise. First, the constant is a clinical judgment: $L_{\text{UB}}$ is specified by the sponsor, informed but not determined by prior evidence (Section~\ref{sec:inference}), so conclusions drawn from $\Delta_{\max}$, or from the comparison of $L^*$ with $L_{\text{UB}}$, are conditional on it; reporting $L^*$ leaves the comparison open to any alternative bound. Second, an $L_{\text{clinical}}$ taken from an estimated average interaction slope is a lower bound on the Lipschitz constant rather than the constant itself; the safety factor of Section~\ref{sec:inference} is a judgment of the same kind as margin discounting, and the framework does not supply its value. Third, for a CATE that changes steeply over a narrow range of the effect modifier---a threshold or step function---the Lipschitz constant is large and equation~(\ref{eq:heterogeneity_bound}) is correspondingly loose. This is by design: the bound is a worst case over the CATEs compatible with the stated $L_{\text{clinical}}$, and a loose bound overstates rather than understates the potential difference; the Kolmogorov--Smirnov alternative for that case is stated in Section~\ref{sec:nabcd_metric}. The requirement is also local: since $\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2 = -\int (F_1 - F_2)\,d\tau$, the bound holds with $L_{\text{clinical}}$ replaced by the Lipschitz constant of $\tau$ on $\{x : F_1(x) \neq F_2(x)\}$, so saturation of the CATE outside that range does not loosen it; for distributions with full support, such as normal distributions, that range is the whole line. Finally, the bound is per effect modifier: it assumes the CATE varies with that modifier alone. When the CATE is additive across modifiers, $\tau(x) = \sum_k \tau_k(x_k)$, the per-modifier bounds combine as $|\bar{\tau}_1 - \bar{\tau}_2| \leq \sum_k L_k W_1^{(k)}$, and each per-modifier $\Delta_{\max}$ is the contribution attributable to that modifier; under interaction between modifiers they do not, because marginal distances do not control the joint distance.
620: Beyond the scope and methodological extensions noted above, two further directions warrant investigation. The upstream identification of which baseline characteristics constitute relevant effect modifiers---which requires evidence on treatment-by-covariate interaction rather than on prognosis alone (Section~\ref{sec:inference})---is itself a non-trivial problem deserving dedicated treatment; the present framework then quantifies distributional distance on the retained modifiers. Bias-correction methods tailored to the boundary behavior of $\widehat{W}_1$ at small samples could extend the operational range of inference.
623: The principal contribution of this paper is a distributional similarity measure for effect modifiers whose heterogeneity bound is attained and whose calibration constant is a per-unit clinical slope. The $W_1$ distance, combined with bootstrap confidence intervals and clinical calibration via $\Delta_{\max}$ or $L^*$, supplies a reproducible quantitative basis for the judgment of ``similar enough'' that ICH E17 describes, operationalizing the similarity of effect modifier distributions, and informing, not prescribing, pooling decisions.

661: \bmsubsection{Non-negativity and Identity of Indiscernibles\label{app:proof1}}
663: Non-negativity is immediate: $W_1(F_1, F_2) = \int |F_1(x) - F_2(x)| \, dx \geq 0$, since the integrand is non-negative.
665: For the identity of indiscernibles, we show both directions. If $F_1 = F_2$, then $|F_1(x) - F_2(x)| = 0$ for all $x$, so $W_1(F_1, F_2) = 0$. Conversely, suppose $W_1(F_1, F_2) = \int |F_1(x) - F_2(x)| \, dx = 0$. Because the integrand is non-negative, it vanishes for Lebesgue-almost every $x$, so $F_1 = F_2$ almost everywhere. Right-continuity then upgrades this to equality everywhere: a null set contains no interval, so for any $x$ and every $\delta > 0$ the interval $(x, x + \delta)$ contains points at which $F_1 = F_2$; choosing such $x_j \downarrow x$ and letting $j \to \infty$ gives $F_1(x) = F_2(x)$. Hence $F_1 = F_2$.
667: \bmsubsection{Asymptotic Properties\label{app:asymptotic}}
669: Under standard regularity conditions, the empirical Wasserstein-1 distance $\widehat{W}_1 = W_1(\hat{F}_1, \hat{F}_2)$ is a consistent estimator of $W_1(F_1, F_2)$.\cite{delbarrio1999}
673: In one dimension, the empirical $W_1$ distance converges at rate $O(n^{-1/2})$ if and only if $\int \sqrt{F(x)(1-F(x))}\,dx < \infty$.\cite{delbarrio1999} For two samples with $n_1/(n_1+n_2) \to \lambda \in (0,1)$, when $\int \sqrt{F_r(1-F_r)}\,dx < \infty$ ($r = 1, 2$) and $\{x : F_1(x) = F_2(x) \in (0,1)\}$ has Lebesgue measure zero, the directional derivative of $W_1$ at $(F_1, F_2)$ is linear, which is the condition under which the bootstrap statement below holds;\cite{fang2019} this condition excludes $F_1 = F_2$. At the boundary $F_1 = F_2$ the derivative is non-linear and the limit is not Gaussian; separately, a percentile interval cannot contain zero, which is the degenerate coverage observed under the null (S1; Section~\ref{sec:sim_results}). For an effect modifier recorded on a grid, the CDFs are step functions and the condition fails whenever they agree at any grid point inside the support; the finite-space results of Sommerfeld and Munk then apply.\cite{sommerfeld2018} Their limit, a maximum over the dual solutions, reduces to a Gaussian when the dual solution is unique, which fails if the two population CDFs coincide on some interval between consecutive grid values. Because the condition concerns the population CDFs, it cannot be checked from the samples; for a distance unresolved against its null floor, such as R2 on systolic blood pressure in Section~\ref{sec:application}, $F_1 = F_2$ is not excluded and the interval is read at its upper limit only (Section~\ref{sec:app_operability}).
677: Under the same conditions the percentile bootstrap confidence interval achieves asymptotically correct coverage;\cite{fang2019} where the two CDFs coincide on an interval, including $F_1 = F_2$, the naive bootstrap is not consistent.\cite{dumbgen1993,fang2019}
679: \bmsection{R Code for $W_1$ Computation\label{app:rcode}}
681: \begin{lstlisting}[caption={R function for computing the Wasserstein-1 distance with bootstrap confidence intervals.},label=lst:nabcd,basicstyle=\fontsize{8}{10}\selectfont\ttfamily]
682: compute_W1 <- function(x1, x2) {
683:   pooled <- c(x1, x2)
684:   all_vals <- sort(unique(pooled))
685:   F1 <- ecdf(x1); F2 <- ecdf(x2)
686:   w1 <- sum(diff(all_vals) *
687:     abs(F1(all_vals[-length(all_vals)]) -
688:         F2(all_vals[-length(all_vals)])))
689:   w1
690: }
692: W1_bootstrap <- function(x1, x2,
693:     B = 2000, conf = 0.95) {
694:   obs <- compute_W1(x1, x2)
695:   boot_vals <- replicate(B, {
696:     b1 <- sample(x1, replace = TRUE)
697:     b2 <- sample(x2, replace = TRUE)
698:     compute_W1(b1, b2)
699:   })
700:   alpha <- 1 - conf
701:   ci <- quantile(boot_vals,
702:     c(alpha/2, 1 - alpha/2), na.rm = TRUE)
703:   list(estimate = obs,
704:        ci_lower = ci[1], ci_upper = ci[2])
705: }
706: \end{lstlisting}
