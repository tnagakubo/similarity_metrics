1               INTRODUCTION

Multi-regional clinical trials (MRCTs), conducted across multiple countries or regulatory regions under a single protocol, have
become a common design in global pharmaceutical development. 1,2 The International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) E17
guideline, adopted in 2017, established principles for planning and designing MRCTs to evaluate whether treatment effects can
be regarded as generalizable across the target population. 3
   Regulatory authorities in Japan and China examine the consistency of treatment effects in regional subpopulations, 4,5 yet
individual regional sample sizes in global MRCTs are often insufficient for that purpose. Regional pooling, in which regions with
similar patient characteristics are grouped for analysis, is a key strategy for enhancing the assessment of regional consistency, and
the ICH E17 guideline describes pooling decisions in terms of the similarity of effect modifier distributions. 3 In the PREVENT
trial of secukinumab, a workshop report describes countries with a similar distribution of effect modifiers being clustered post
hoc into five pooled regions on binary characteristics (CRP and MRI positivity, sex). 4

   An effect modifier is a baseline patient characteristic, such as age, disease severity or a genetic marker, for which the treatment
benefit differs across subgroups. Regions with different patient compositions may therefore observe different average treatment
effects even if the drug works identically at the individual level, so the similarity of the full effect modifier distribution, not only
its average level, matters for regional pooling. We focus on continuous effect modifiers; categorical effect modifiers raise distinct
considerations and are outside the present scope.
   Despite the regulatory importance of effect modifier distributional similarity, current practice lacks a standardized, clinically
calibrated methodology. The ICH E17 guideline provides no specific metric, threshold, or statistical procedure for determining
when distributions are “similar enough.” Song et al., on the NMPA’s implementation of ICH E17, identify the pooling strategy
as an area where ambiguity remains and describe clustering populations on distances defined from the correlation between
effect modifiers and endpoints. 5 The pooling-strategy chapter of a reference volume on multi-regional trials under ICH E17
introduces, as one of many possible approaches, cluster analysis of regions on region-level summaries of the candidate effect
modifiers, and does not evaluate it. 6 Two gaps remain. First, to our knowledge no distance between regional effect modifier
distributions has been proposed and evaluated for this purpose: a single summary per modifier leaves two regions that agree on
it but differ in spread, shape, or tail behaviour at distance zero, and any finite set of summaries requires deciding in advance
which distributional features are relevant. Second, the chapter calls for a judgment of whether differences in the impact of effect
modifiers across pooled regions are “clinically meaningful or not” but supplies no metric to operationalize that judgment.
   The ingredients are not new in themselves. The inequality on which the clinical calibration rests is the Kantorovich–Rubinstein
duality, used in the same form in the optimal-transport analysis of domain adaptation; 7,8 the similarity between a trial sample
and a target population has been summarized by propensity-score metrics, 9 a generalizability index 10 and an equivalence test
on a trimmed Mallows distance. 11 None of these converts the difference between two regions’ distributions of an individual
effect modifier into a bound on the regional effect difference whose constant is a clinical slope, or evaluates such a distance for
regional pooling. The contribution of this paper is that formulation and the evaluation of the measure’s identification and of its
estimator’s properties.
   This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a quantitative framework for assessing effect modifier distributional similarity
across regions, based on the Wasserstein-1 (W1 ) distance between two cumulative distribution functions, retained in the original
measurement units of the effect modifier. We estimate W1 with bootstrap confidence intervals and link it to clinical interpretation
through a calibration framework.

2.1          Existing Approaches and Their Limitations

The most widely used measure for comparing covariate distributions across groups is the standardized mean difference (SMD), 12
which by construction captures only differences in location: N(50, 52 ) and N(50, 152 ) yield SMD = 0 despite a threefold
difference in spread. Distributional distance measures compare distributions beyond summary statistics: empirical distribution
function (EDF) statistics such as the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic, DKS = supx |F1 (x) − F2 (x)|, compare CDFs directly,
and information-theoretic divergences such as Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence take a density-based approach.
   Each of these approaches has limitations for comparing effect modifier distributions across regions. EDF-based statistics such
as the KS statistic capture full distributional differences but lack a clinically interpretable scale, and the bound on treatment
effect heterogeneity they admit is governed by the total variation of the CATE, a constant that is clinically stated only for a

2.2         Per-Effect-Modifier Wasserstein-1 Distance

Among distributional distances that could capture the full distributional differences relevant to regional pooling, we adopt the
Wasserstein-1 distance (W1 ) for three reasons: it responds to differences beyond location; the heterogeneity bound it admits
(Proposition 1 below) has a per-unit clinical slope Lclinical as its constant; and that bound is valid for every CATE in the class,
sharp when the slope is the only restriction placed on the CATE, and tighter, as a function of W1 and the mean difference, when
the CATE is also monotone; for any particular CATE it is therefore a conservative upper bound. The W1 distance admits three
equivalent representations on the real line. 13,14

2.4          Interpretation and Clinical Calibration

We refer to the Lipschitz constant of the CATE function τ (x) as the clinical slope Lclinical : the maximum change in the treatment
effect per unit change in the effect modifier, on the outcome scale of the trial—for example, absolute 30-day mortality per year of
QUANTIFYING EFFECT MODIFIER SIMILARITY FOR REGIONAL POOLING                                                                                 5


age or per mmHg of systolic blood pressure, quoted as a proportion, so that Lclinical = 1 × 10−2 per year is one percentage point
per year and a margin ∆clin stated in percentage points is divided by 100. Meta-regression on treatment-by-covariate interaction
in prior individual patient data (IPD) estimates the average interaction slope, 20,21 which is, in absolute value, a lower bound on
the Lipschitz constant, so an Lclinical specified from it should carry a sponsor-specified safety factor.
   From Proposition 1, the maximum potential difference in regional average treatment effects attributable to effect modifier
distributional differences is
                                                     ∆max = Lclinical · W1 (F1 , F2 ),                                           (6)
expressed in the units of the trial outcome scale; when Lclinical is independently known, equation (6) provides a direct clinical-scale
translation of the observed distributional difference.
   The estimator W   b 1 has a percentile bootstrap CI (Section 2.3), and inferential uncertainty propagates linearly to ∆max when
Lclinical is treated as fixed: [∆max,L , ∆max,U ] = Lclinical · [W1,L , W1,U ]. When Lclinical itself is uncertain or unavailable, the sponsor
states instead a plausible upper bound LUB on it from prior evidence and a pre-specified clinical margin ∆clin (the minimal
clinically important treatment effect difference, the non-inferiority margin, or the targeted overall effect). By equation (6), a
common CATE whose slope does not exceed LUB can produce a regional difference of at least ∆clin only if W1 ≥ ∆clin /LUB , and
within the class of CATEs restricted only by their slope some such CATE produces it whenever W1 ≥ ∆clin /LUB (Appendix A.2);
we call ∆clin /LUB the reference distance, the sponsor’s illustrative input rather than a threshold for pooling, and read each W           b1
                                                                ′                                     ′
with its confidence interval against it; any other bound LUB       is read in the same way at ∆clin /LUB , since the distance and its
interval are reported. An interval wholly below this distance means that, provided Lclinical ≤ LUB , the distributional difference
cannot produce such a regional difference even at the upper confidence limit of the distance.
   Specifying Lclinical (or its upper bound LUB ) is a clinical judgment, informed but not determined by prior evidence, for which
the sponsor carries responsibility—the same footing as a non-inferiority margin. A prognostic gradient alone guarantees no
bound on the modification slope, prognostic and treatment-effect gradients being distinct quantities. 22,23
   The framework is deliberately estimation-centered rather than testing-based: W1 has no universal cutoff that separates
“similar” from “dissimilar,” and what a given W1 means clinically depends on the clinical slope Lclinical , which is external to the
distributional comparison. Per-pair W1 and ∆max with bootstrap CIs are inputs for sponsor and regulatory judgment, which is not
driven by a significance test; 24 the one test, the null floor of Section 2.3, governs how an estimate is displayed and read, not
whether regions are pooled.


3          SIMULATION STUDIES

Two simulation studies follow, both reported in the ADEMP structure for simulation studies. 25 The estimation study (Section 3.1)
evaluates bias, variability and bootstrap coverage of W b 1 . The identification study (Section 3.2) evaluates whether each distance
measure can detect, at some sample size, that a candidate region’s effect modifier distribution differs from the anchor’s, on
a selection task in which candidates built from the anchor’s population are ranked against candidates built from a different
population, with the number of true matches supplied. It does not order regions that all differ from the anchor, nor rank them by
effect difference, which depends on the unknown CATE: under a linear CATE only the mean difference matters, whereas under
the V-shaped CATE τ (x) = Lclinical |x − 50| a scale difference about the centre 50 produces the full Lclinical W1 while the SMD is
zero.

3.2              Identification

3.2.1                Design

Four worlds are simulated, each with regions constructed either from the anchor’s own population (true matches) or from a
discordant one. Set 1 (Gaussian) places discordance in location and scale; Set 2 (log-normal) in location and in dispersion
combined with shape; Set 3 (mixture) in distributional shape alone, every region, matched or discordant, having mean 50 and SD
10. Set 4 (extremes) places a 5% subgroup in each tail, 40 units from the centre in the anchor; discordant regions displace both
subgroups (to 70 or 100 units), raise their prevalence to 12% (mean fixed), or displace only the upper subgroup to 100 units,
and two control regions instead shift their whole population by 2 and 3 units. In the subgroup cells a subgroup of prevalence ε
displaced by d moves W1 by εd but the KS statistic by at most about ε; the two control regions shift the whole population and
contain no displaced subgroup. Ground truth is the construction label, so the target is defined without reference to any of the
compared measures and the comparison is not circular.

3.2.2                Two Behaviours: Blind and Recovering

Extending the sample-size grid to n = 2,000 separates the measures into two behaviours (Figure 3). A measure is blind in a
cell when its AUC stays at chance through n = 2,000: the population value of its distance carries no information about the
construction label, so the task is unsolvable for that measure at any sample size, an identification failure and not a power failure.
This is exact by construction in every mean-matched cell, where the population SMD is zero. On the selection task, SMD is
blind in 7 of the 13 cells (marked in Table 4; AUC 0.49–0.51 across the full grid in all of them).
   A measure is recovering when its AUC reaches the target and stays above it at every larger sample size: identification succeeds,
and what remains is a required-sample-size question. W1 and the KS statistic are recovering in every cell; the KS statistic is
blind nowhere, reaching selection AUC 0.98 at n = 2,000 even in its weakest cell, asymmetric severity in Set 4.
   Table 4 reports, for all 13 cells, the per-region sample size at which each measure’s selection AUC reaches 0.9, with 95%
Monte Carlo intervals. The sample sizes of W1 and the KS statistic are not compared here; the reason to use W1 (Section 5) is
that it is not blind where the SMD is and carries a clinical-scale translation. A check of the bulk-shift control, the cell in which
the KS statistic reaches the target at the smaller n (Table 4), registered after an exploratory look, is not reported here (protocol
and outputs in the repository).

4.1          Scenario Design and Effect Modifier Selection

The sponsor takes age and systolic blood pressure (SBP) as the candidate effect modifiers for Drug T. The Fibrinolytic Therapy
Trialists’ (FTT) meta-analysis of existing fibrinolytic agents 27 concluded that, among patients presenting with ST elevation or
bundle-branch block, benefit is present irrespective of age and of blood pressure, and reports no per-unit CATE slope on the
absolute mortality scale for either; we therefore treat Lclinical as unknown for both and read each distance against the reference
distance ∆clin /LUB of Section 2.4. The illustrative bounds are LUB,age = 1 × 10−2 per year and LUB,SBP = 2 × 10−3 per mmHg
on the 30-day mortality scale, set so that at a margin ∆clin of 1 percentage point the reference distances are 1 year of age and
5 mmHg of SBP; the FTT meta-analysis 27 and the GUSTO-I prognostic model 28 are reference points, not sources of the bounds.
   The sponsor selects Region 8 as the anchor region (n = 2,916 patients). Records with SBP = 0 mmHg (6 patients) or age
> 100 years (3 patients) were retained; excluding them changed no null-floor classification below and 2 adjacent SBP ranks,
and winsorizing at the 1st and 99th percentiles changed 4 of the 30 ranks; both checks were made post hoc. SBP is heaped on
multiples of 10 mmHg, most in Region 8 (79.5% of readings), so recording practice may contribute to the SBP distances (not
quantified here).


4.2          Null Floor: Which Distances Are Distinguishable from No Difference?

We computed the null floor of Section 2.3 separately for each candidate partner, from two independent resamples, with
replacement, of Region 8’s (R8) empirical distribution at sizes nR8 = 2,916 and npartner , recomputing W b 1 over 2,000 repetitions.
We call an observed distance unresolved when it lies at or below the 95th percentile of its null distribution (a one-sided test of
F1 = F2 at level 0.05). A permutation test of F1 = F2 on the pooled sample classifies all 30 distances in the same way (30 of
30 agree). On age, the distances of six of the fifteen partners—R1, R4, R5, R7, R9 and R15—are unresolved at level 0.05; on
systolic blood pressure, fourteen of fifteen are resolved, the single exception being R2.
   How large the differences of the six partners unresolved at level 0.05 could be is read from the upper confidence limits
(Figure 4, Table 5), which for small distances near the boundary err above rather than below the true value in the estimation study
(Section 3.1.2). The GUSTO-I distances are 0.03–0.30 SD of Region 8’s distributions, below the 0.4 SD at which S3–S7 begin
and in places above the 0.2 SD of S2; the extension cells at 0.2 SD, the application’s operating point (Table 3), gave percentile
coverage 0.951 and 0.947 and upper-limit coverage 0.9825 and 0.9775.

5                          DISCUSSION

This paper answers the requirements set out in Section 2.1. W1 meets requirement (i): in the identification study the SMD is at
chance at every sample size examined in the mean-matched cells, whereas W1 , like the KS statistic, reaches the discrimination
target in every cell. It meets requirements (ii) and (iii): the heterogeneity bound ∆max (equation 6) translates distributional
distance into a clinically interpretable scale, is sharp when the slope is the only restriction on the CATE and tightens under
12                                                                                                                     AUTHOR ONE ET AL .

monotonicity (Appendix A.2), and its constant is a clinical slope that the sponsor specifies. Its estimation is characterized in
Section 3.1.2: for true distances of about 0.4 to 1.2 SD at n ≥ 100 per region, percentile bootstrap coverage was below 0.95
beyond Monte Carlo error in 5 of the ten cells.
   These findings carry two implications for pooling assessments, illustrated by the GUSTO-I example (Section 4). First, when
several candidate effect modifiers are considered, the per-modifier distances need not agree: a partner close to the anchor on
one effect modifier can be distant on another, as in the contrast between R2 and R9. Second, within one effect modifier ∆max
multiplies every distance by the same Lclinical , so calibration does not reorder partners; what the unknown-slope reading adds is
the reference distance ∆clin /LUB specified for that modifier, against which each distance is read.
   Across the 15 partners the orderings by age and by systolic blood pressure distance show no positive agreement (Spearman
correlation −0.30, 95% interval −0.51 to 0.02 from 1,000 patient-level bootstrap replicates that resample within every region,
Region 8 shared across partners); the interval excludes the strong positive agreement under which one modifier’s distances
could stand in for the other’s, so a single distance aggregated over the two modifiers could mask differences that a per-modifier
assessment retains.
   In GUSTO-I, whose regional differences are predominantly differences in location (partner standard deviations within a factor
of 1.11 of the anchor’s), W1 ordered the 15 partners much as the SMD did (Spearman correlation 0.90, 95% interval 0.70 to 0.99,
for age and 0.95, 0.90 to 0.99, for systolic blood pressure), and a registered survey of region pairs in GUSTO-I, IST-1 and IST-3
found agreement of the same order (Spearman correlation 0.839–0.996; repository). 29,30 The limitations of the SMD bind where
distributional differences go beyond location—mixtures and rare displaced subgroups—and configurations beyond location
cannot be excluded before the distances are computed. The reason to use W1 is that, like the KS statistic, it is not blind where the
SMD is, and that it carries a translation to the clinical scale whose constant is a per-unit clinical slope, valid for every CATE in
the class and conservative for any particular one.
   Because W1 requires only baseline effect modifier distributions, regional data can be assembled from prior trials, registries,
electronic health records, or other real-world evidence (RWE) sources, provided the source population approximates the
population each region is expected to enrol, so that the assessment can be made at the planning stage. It uses neither treatment
assignment nor outcomes, requires individual-level baseline values, and complements rather than replaces assessments based on
observed treatment effects.
   The W1 distance is one input within a holistic regulatory evaluation. 3,4 It treats continuous effect modifiers marginally, leaving
categorical and multivariate extensions for future work, and it is stated for collapsible effect scales (Section 2). Which baseline
characteristics are effect modifiers is taken as given; their identification requires evidence on treatment-by-covariate interaction
rather than on prognosis alone (Section 2.4) and lies outside the framework. Because W1 is non-negative, W           b 1 exhibits positive
bias near zero, zero percentile coverage when the true value is zero (S1), and undercoverage near the boundary and where the
bias is large relative to the sampling standard deviation, with the intervals erring above the true value (Section 3.1.2). 31 The
coverage reported for the estimation study from n = 100 per region refers to true distances of about 0.4 to 1.2 SD of the reference
distribution, with equal regional sizes; the registered extension (Table 3) covers 0.2 SD at the application’s sample sizes, unequal
sizes, a heavy-tailed reference and grid-recorded data, but not every combination the application presents. Proposition 1 assumes
a CATE common to all regions, so regional differences in extrinsic factors that alter τ itself lie outside what W1 measures, and
unmeasured effect modifiers may contribute to heterogeneity beyond what the distance captures.
   The limitations of the calibration are those of the Lipschitz premise of Proposition 1. First, the constant is a clinical judgment
(Section 2.4), so conclusions drawn from ∆max , or from the reference distance ∆clin /LUB , are conditional on it. Second, for a
CATE that changes steeply over a narrow range of the effect modifier—a threshold or step function—the Lipschitz constant is
large and equation (4) is correspondingly loose. This is by design: the bound is a worst case over the CATEs compatible with
the stated Lclinical , and a loose bound overstates rather than understates the potential difference. Finally, the bound is per effect
                                                               P
modifier. If the CATE is Lipschitz with respect to d(x, y) = k Lk |xk − yk |, then |τ̄1 − τ̄2 | ≤ Wd (P1 , P2 ), the Wasserstein distance

   Inference tailored to the boundary behavior of W  b 1 —resampling fewer than n observations 32 or the numerical delta method for
directionally differentiable functionals 33 —could extend the operational range of the intervals at small distances.
   The principal contribution of this paper is a distributional similarity measure for effect modifiers whose heterogeneity bound
holds for every CATE with a given per-unit clinical slope, is sharp over that class and conservative for any particular CATE, and
whose calibration constant is that slope. With bootstrap confidence intervals and clinical calibration via ∆max or the reference

A.2 Comparison with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov Bound; Attainment on the Outcome Scale
